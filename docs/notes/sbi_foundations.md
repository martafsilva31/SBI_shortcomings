*Theory note for the SBI shortcomings project: why the Asimov dataset is trusted, the conditions under which that trust is earned, why a neural per-event likelihood can forfeit those conditions while a histogram need not, and what can be done about it. ## 1. The question this project asks

Before an experiment runs, physicists want to know what sensitivity to *expect*. The gold-standard answer is brute force: simulate many pseudo-experiments ("toys"), analyse each exactly as real data would be analysed, and report the median of the resulting measures — median significance, median limit, median likelihood-scan width. The convenient answer is to analyse a single artificial dataset in which every observable is set exactly to its expectation value — the **Asimov dataset** — and read the same measures off that one fit. The convenience rests entirely on a claimed equivalence: *the Asimov result equals the median of the toys.*

This project asks whether that equivalence survives when the analysis is a **neural simulation-based inference (NSBI)** analysis in a low-statistics regime. The operational form of the claim tested here — the project's working definition of the **Asimov property** — is:

> the $-2\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}$ scan curve computed on the Asimov dataset equals, pointwise in $\mu$, the median of the scan curves of an ensemble of toy pseudo-experiments.

The founding observation (Tei's demonstration, first team meeting) is qualitative and simple to state: a **histogram** analysis of off-shell Higgs production satisfies this property at every luminosity tried, while an **NSBI** analysis of *the same events* violates it at low luminosity — the toy median comes out systematically *steeper* than the Asimov curve — with the two converging only at very high luminosity, where the exactly-known Poisson rate term dominates the likelihood. Same events, same toy recipe, same comparator; the only thing that changes is the analysis method. §11 shows the behaviour schematically; §§2–10 develop the machinery needed to explain it.

Two properties of the failure set the stakes, and both come from the team's discussion of the result. It is a failure of **confidence, not of bias**: the NSBI fit does not necessarily land on a wrong $\mu$, but on a reasonable $\mu$ while claiming a precision it does not have, and nothing in the fit output signals this. And it is not fundamentally about luminosity: **any rare process is a low-statistics process**, whatever the integrated luminosity — a 10 fb⁻¹ analysis of a rare signal is statistically a 1 fb⁻¹ analysis of a common one. Whatever breaks here breaks for every analysis whose *signal* sample is small, which is precisely the regime NSBI is advertised for.

## 2. The physics stage and the samples

### 2.1 The process

The test case is off-shell Higgs production at parton level:

$$
gg \to H^* \to ZZ \to 4\ell
$$

Two amplitudes lead to the same four-lepton final state: the Higgs-mediated one (a quark loop producing an off-shell Higgs which decays to a $Z$ pair) and the continuum background (a quark box producing the $Z$ pair directly). Both are **loop-induced** — there is no tree-level $gg \to ZZ$ — so they are of comparable size and they **interfere**. Above the $2m_Z$ threshold the Higgs is far off shell, the signal amplitude is small, and the interference with the box is **destructive**.

The parameter of interest is the signal-strength modifier $\mu$, defined so that $\mu = 1$ is the Standard Model. It scales the signal *amplitude* by $\sqrt{\mu}$; the signal-squared term therefore scales as $\mu$ and the signal–background cross term — the interference — as $\sqrt{\mu}$. That square root is the origin of most of what is statistically peculiar about this model (§7).

### 2.2 The probability model

Write $\nu_S, \nu_I, \nu_B$ for the component yields and $p_S, p_I, p_B$ for the component densities (each density normalised to 1; the interference "density" is signed, with $\nu_I < 0$). The per-event density under the signal+background+interference (SBI) hypothesis is the yield-weighted mixture:

$$
p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)=\frac{\mu\,\nu_S\,p_S(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_I\,p_I(x)+\nu_B\,p_B(x)}{\mu\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_I+\nu_B}
$$

Because a signed density cannot be sampled from directly, the code uses an algebraically equivalent re-arrangement in terms of the *physical* SM mixture $p_{\rm SBI} \equiv p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu{=}1)$, which avoids negative weights:

$$
p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)=\frac{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S\,p_S(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}\,p_{\rm SBI}(x)+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B\,p_B(x)}{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}
$$

The two forms are identical: expanding $\nu_{\rm SBI}\,p_{\rm SBI} = \nu_S p_S + \nu_I p_I + \nu_B p_B$ in the numerator and collecting terms recovers the first. The corresponding **total rate** — the quantity the Poisson term of §10 needs — is

$$
\nu_{\rm tot}(\mu)=L\left(\mu\,\sigma_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\sigma_I+\sigma_B\right)
$$

with $L$ the integrated luminosity and $\sigma$ the component cross-sections.

### 2.3 Yields: the numbers that set the regime

Expected yields at $\mu = 1$ (from [context.md](../context.md); the 1 fb⁻¹ column *is* the cross-section in fb):

| Component | 1 fb⁻¹ | 10 fb⁻¹ | 100 fb⁻¹ | 1000 fb⁻¹ |
|-----------|--------|---------|----------|-----------|
| SIG ($\sigma_S$) | 0.310  | 3.099   | 30.990   | 309.897   |
| BKG ($\sigma_B$) | 5.953  | 59.530  | 595.303  | 5953.034  |
| INT ($\sigma_I$) | −0.606 | −6.064  | −60.643  | −606.433  |
| SBI total | 5.656  | 56.565  | 565.650  | 5656.498  |

Two features of this table matter later:

- **The headline regime, 1 fb⁻¹, is an experiment expecting ≈ 5.7 events in total.** This is the number meant whenever this note says "~6 events". It is the *total* S+B+I count; the signal's net contribution is **negative** ($0.310 - 0.606 = -0.296$), because destructive interference exceeds the tiny signal-squared term.
- **The interference is large compared with the signal** ($\lvert\sigma_I\rvert \approx 2\sigma_S$). This breaks a regularity condition in §7.

### 2.4 What is in the samples

The events are **parton-level MC**: a Monte Carlo generator produced fixed-order $gg \to 4\ell$ events — no parton shower, no hadronisation, no detector simulation. Each stored event carries six four-vectors (two purely longitudinal incoming partons and the four final-state leptons) and the **squared matrix elements** for each component (`msq_sig`, `msq_int`, `msq_bkg`, `msq_sbi`). Those matrix elements are what allow a single sample to serve *every* value of $\mu$, by per-event reweighting:

$$
w(\mu)=w_{\rm SM}\cdot\frac{\mu\,{\rm msq}_{\rm sig}+\sqrt{\mu}\,{\rm msq}_{\rm int}+{\rm msq}_{\rm bkg}}{{\rm msq}_{\rm sbi}}
$$

Reweighting works because every component hypothesis populates the *same* phase space: changing $\mu$ changes how much each phase-space point weighs, never which points exist. The samples also carry 45 BSM matrix-element variations on a $(c_6, c_t, c_g) = 5\times3\times3$ EFT grid — the same trick, pointed at a later phase of the project. One kinematic consequence of the fixed-order $gg$ initial state matters for the dimension counting below: the four-lepton system has exactly zero transverse momentum, since there is no initial-state radiation to recoil against.

### 2.5 What the analyses actually see

| Analysis | Observable(s) | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| Histogram | $m_{4\ell}$ (the four-lepton invariant mass) | 1 |
| NSBI | the four leptons' kinematics: $(p_T, \eta, \phi, E)$ for each of $\ell_1 \dots \ell_4$ | 16 network inputs |

The **16** quoted throughout this project is the **input width of the neural networks**: four leptons × four coordinates. It is not the dimension of the physical phase space, and §§7–8 turn on the distinction. The coordinates are redundant: for massless leptons $E$ is fixed by $(p_T, \eta)$, removing 4; the vanishing four-lepton transverse momentum (§2.4) removes 2; overall azimuthal invariance removes 1. What remains is **≈ 9 genuine degrees of freedom** — the size of the standard four-lepton basis (five decay angles plus $m(Z_1)$, $m(Z_2)$, $m_{4\ell}$, $y_{4\ell}$). The networks are therefore fed 16 redundant coordinates spanning about 9 real dimensions. Nine is still large compared with 5.7 events, so every argument below survives; but the phase-space dimension is 9, not 16.

## 3. Weighted events, and how toy pseudo-experiments are made

The comparator used throughout this note — the median of an ensemble of toys — inherits its meaning from the construction given in this section.

### 3.1 What a weighted MC sample is

A weighted sample is a list of $N_{\rm MC}$ simulated events, each carrying kinematics $x_i$ and a weight $w_i \ge 0$, normalised so that the weights *sum to the physics expectation*:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm MC}} w_i \cdot L=\nu_{\rm tot} \quad\text{(the expected event count at luminosity } L\text{)}
$$

The product $w_i L$ is the mean number of times the simulated configuration $x_i$ would occur in an actual experiment of luminosity $L$. Typically $w_i L \ll 1$: the simulation covers phase space much more densely than a real dataset would populate it. That is the point of weighted MC — dense coverage of *shape*, with the *rate* carried by the normalisation.

### 3.2 The recipe

A toy pseudo-experiment is generated by **one independent Poisson draw per event**:

$$
w_i^{\rm toy}\sim\mathcal{P}(\lambda=w_i\,L)\qquad\text{independently for each } i
$$

The toy *is* the collection of events with $w_i^{\rm toy} > 0$, each counted with its drawn multiplicity. Most events draw 0 and vanish; some draw 1; occasionally one draws 2. Figure 1 (left) shows the weights as grey stems and one toy as blue dots; (right) the same construction seen through a histogram — the Asimov template (weights summed per bin) with three toys around it.

![Toy generation: per-event Poisson draws (left) and the binned view (right)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/toy_mechanics.png)

### 3.3 Exactness of the recipe

The recipe is an exact sample from the extended likelihood. The proof rests on three properties of the Poisson distribution.

**Step 1 — the model is a superposition of independent point processes.** "Events of type $x_i$ occur at rate $w_i L$" defines, for each $i$, an independent Poisson process; the physical dataset is their superposition. Drawing $w_i^{\rm toy} \sim \mathcal{P}(w_i L)$ independently per event is a literal simulation of that statement.

**Step 2 — the total rate comes out right.** A sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson with the summed mean:

$$
N_{\rm toy}=\sum_i w_i^{\rm toy}\sim\mathcal{P}\Big(\sum_i w_i L\Big)=\mathcal{P}(\nu_{\rm tot})
$$

The toy's total event count therefore fluctuates exactly as the extended likelihood's rate term requires.

**Step 3 — the shape comes out right.** Conditional on the total count $N_{\rm toy}$, the drawn events are distributed over the sample in proportion to their weights (the thinning property of Poisson processes) — that is, as $N_{\rm toy}$ i.i.d. draws from the normalised density that the weighted sample represents. This is the shape term of the extended likelihood.

One Poisson draw per weight is therefore one realisation of $\mathcal{P}(n|\nu)\prod_i p(x_i)$. Rate and shape fluctuate **coherently and simultaneously**: a toy with an upward count fluctuation has it in *specific places*, not spread uniformly. No separate machinery is required for the two terms, and there is no double counting.

### 3.4 What toys are *not*

- **Not a bootstrap.** The bootstrap resamples the observed dataset with replacement, holding $N$ fixed at the observed count; it estimates the sampling variability of a statistic *given the data obtained*. Toys sample fresh datasets *from the model*, count and all. For coverage and Asimov-vs-median questions the model's own fluctuations are the object of study, so toys are the right tool and the bootstrap is not.
- **Not an estimate of model or training uncertainty.** Toys fluctuate the *data*; they hold the analysis — including any trained network — perfectly fixed. As put in the April meeting: *toys estimate the statistical uncertainty of the data; ensembling estimates the bias and variance of the network — a different uncertainty entirely.* §13 returns to what ensembles do.
- **Not luminosity-agnostic.** The draw is from $\mathcal{P}(w_i L)$: the same weighted sample yields honest 1 fb⁻¹ toys (sparse, mostly empty) and honest 1000 fb⁻¹ toys (dense) just by changing $L$. This is what makes a sweep in $L$ possible without regenerating anything.

### 3.5 The protocol, and the definition of the comparator

For each toy, the full analysis (histogram or NSBI) produces a $-2\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}$ scan over the $\mu$ grid; the comparator is the **pointwise-per-$\mu$ median** of the toy curves, with a quantile band, overlaid on the Asimov curve.

> ⚠️ "The median of the toys" is a definition, not a given. At least three inequivalent objects deserve the name: (1) the pointwise median of the toy curves referenced to a common baseline; (2) the pointwise median after each toy is min-subtracted (referenced to its own $\hat{\mu}$ — the convention that matches the scalar-statistic theorem of §5, since $t_\mu$ is defined relative to $\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mu})$); (3) the median of a *scalar summary* of each toy (its interval width, say), which is what the Asimov theorem actually addresses. These coincide in the asymptotic limit and need not coincide at six events: a pointwise median of non-parabolic curves is not the curve of any single experiment, and can itself fail to be parabolic. Tei flagged the same subtlety from the normalisation side — individual toy curves can run below the nominal Asimov curve, so the vertical axis is not automatically comparable between toys until a reference is fixed. **Every quantitative claim must name the convention it used.** Which convention is the *interesting* one is an open question (§18).

## 4. Likelihood-ratio inference: Wilks and Wald

The statistical machinery is frequentist and likelihood-based throughout. For a dataset of $n$ observed events with observables $x_1 \dots x_n$, the **extended likelihood** is

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\underbrace{\mathcal{P}\big(n\mid\nu(\mu)\big)}_{\text{rate term}}\;\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(x_i|\mu)}_{\text{shape term}}
$$

and inference uses the likelihood-ratio statistic

$$
t_\mu=-2\ln\lambda(\mu),\qquad\lambda(\mu)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mu)}{\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mu})}
$$

with $\hat{\mu}$ the maximum-likelihood estimate. (There are no nuisance parameters in this project yet; with them, $\lambda$ becomes the profile likelihood ratio and the story is unchanged.)

**Origin of the two theorems.** Expand $\ln\mathcal{L}$ around its maximum. The first derivative vanishes there by definition, so to second order

$$
-2\ln\lambda(\mu)\approx(\mu-\hat{\mu})^2\cdot\Big[-\partial_\mu^2\ln\mathcal{L}\Big]_{\hat{\mu}}=\frac{(\mu-\hat{\mu})^2}{\sigma^2}
$$

which is **Wald's approximation**: the scan curve is a parabola whose curvature *is* the inverse variance of the estimate. If, additionally, $\hat{\mu}$ is Gaussian around the truth with that same $\sigma$ — which a central limit theorem must supply — then $(\mu-\hat{\mu})/\sigma$ is standard normal under the hypothesis $\mu$, and its square, $t_\mu$, follows a **$\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom**. That is **Wilks' theorem**. Every familiar rule follows: $\Delta(-2\ln\mathcal{L}) = 1 \Leftrightarrow$ 68 % interval, $\Delta = 3.84 \Leftrightarrow$ 95 %, significance $= \sqrt{t_0}$.

Both theorems therefore rest on two ingredients: *the log-likelihood is locally quadratic* and *the estimator is Gaussian*. Both are large-sample statements. Figure 2 shows what they buy and what their loss looks like; the right panel is an exact six-event counting experiment — no interference, nothing exotic — in which the parabola is already a poor description across the scan range.

![Asymptotic parabola vs an exact six-event scan](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/wilks_wald.png)

> ⚠️ Neither theorem holds by fiat at 5.7 expected events. Whether its conclusions survive *approximately* is a quantitative question, and it is the question of this project. §7 sets out which conditions must hold: "large sample" has to be unpacked before it can be transported from a histogram to a neural network.

## 5. The Asimov dataset and what is actually proven

The **Asimov dataset** (Cowan, Cranmer, Gross, Vitells 2011, Sec. 3.2) is the artificial dataset in which every observable takes exactly its expected value: every histogram bin holds its expected yield, and the total count equals $\nu(\mu')$ for the assumed truth $\mu'$. Its defining property is that fitting it returns the truth exactly,

$$
\hat{\mu}_A=\mu'
$$

**The median property, derived in three lines.** Work inside the Wald approximation, testing hypothesis $\mu$ when the truth is $\mu'$:

1. $t_\mu \approx (\mu-\hat{\mu})^2/\sigma^2$, and under the truth, $\hat{\mu} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu', \sigma)$.
2. $t_\mu$ is a *monotone function* of $\hat{\mu}$ on either side of $\mu$; a monotone function maps the median of the input to the median of the output. The median of $\hat{\mu}$ is $\mu'$.
3. The median of $t_\mu$ is therefore its value at $\hat{\mu}=\mu'$ — which, by the defining property above, is exactly the Asimov value $t_{\mu,A}=(\mu-\mu')^2/\sigma^2$.

The theorem delivers median expected significance without a single toy, which is why "fit the Asimov, skip the toys" became standard practice in HEP.

> 🎯 **What is proven vs. what we test.** The proven statement is a median property of the *scalar test statistic*, derived *inside* the Wald approximation — step 1 assumed the parabola and the Gaussian estimator before the argument began. The property this project tests — pointwise equality of the whole Asimov *curve* with the median of toy *curves*, at ~6 expected events — is strictly stronger, and it is this study's **own operational definition**; it is not a claim of Cowan et al. Its violation therefore contradicts no theorem. It demonstrates something else: a regime assumed asymptotic is not.

## 6. Coverage: what an error bar actually promises

Everything in this project — the Asimov check, the overconfidence language, the SBI literature of §13, the mitigation designs of §17 — ultimately refers to one property.

### 6.1 The definition

A procedure that turns data into an interval has **coverage** $1-\alpha$ if, under repeated experiments with true value $\mu'$, the reported interval contains $\mu'$ a fraction $1-\alpha$ of the time:

$$
P\big(\mu'\in[\hat{\mu}_{\rm lo},\hat{\mu}_{\rm hi}]\big)=1-\alpha\quad\text{for every }\mu'
$$

Three properties follow. Coverage is a property of the **procedure**, not of any single interval: a given interval either contains the truth or does not, and which is never known. The probability is over *repetitions of the experiment*, which is why toys (§3) are the natural instrument for measuring it. And the promise holds **for every $\mu'$**: a procedure can cover at one truth and fail at another, so coverage must be *mapped*, not spot-checked.

**Vocabulary.** Exact coverage = calibrated. Coverage above nominal = **conservative** (valid but wasteful: intervals longer than necessary). Coverage below nominal = **overconfident** — the failure mode this project is about: intervals shorter than their stated confidence warrants. Figure 3 shows the effect: same central values, intervals reported 45 % too short, and the "68 %" procedure captures the truth less than half the time.

![Calibrated vs overconfident interval procedures](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/coverage.png)

### 6.2 The Neyman construction — coverage with no asymptotics at all

Neyman (1937) showed how to build intervals whose coverage is guaranteed **by construction**, with no appeal to large samples:

1. Choose a test statistic $T(\text{data}; \mu)$.
2. For **each** candidate value of $\mu$, find the acceptance region — the set of $T$ values containing probability $1-\alpha$ *under that* $\mu$ (in practice: throw toys at $\mu$, take the empirical quantiles of $T$).
3. Observe the data; compute $T(\text{data}; \mu)$ for every $\mu$.
4. Report the confidence set: every $\mu$ whose acceptance region contains the observed $T$.

Coverage is then automatic: whatever the truth is, step 2 made its acceptance region contain the data with probability $1-\alpha$, so the truth enters the reported set with probability $1-\alpha$. No parabola, no Gaussianity, no large $N$ — the guarantee is exact, at any sample size, for any statistic. What the choice of statistic and ordering controls is not validity but **efficiency**: poor choices give valid-but-long intervals (the Feldman–Cousins ordering of §6.3 is the standard refinement).

**The connection to the asymptotic rule.** The familiar prescription "cut the scan at $\Delta(-2\ln\mathcal{L}) = 1$" is the Neyman construction with two shortcuts: the statistic fixed to $t_\mu$, and the per-$\mu$ toy quantiles replaced by the $\chi^2$ quantile that Wilks promises. When the asymptotic conditions hold, the shortcut is free. When they fail, the $\chi^2$ quantile is simply the *wrong threshold*, and the full toy-based construction is the fallback that always works, at the price of throwing toys per hypothesis. In these terms, the most robust mitigation in §17 is to stop taking the shortcut.

### 6.3 Feldman–Cousins: which outcomes enter the acceptance region

Step 2 of the recipe above is underdetermined. It fixes the *size* of each acceptance region — probability $1-\alpha$ under its own $\mu$ — but not *which* outcomes of that probability to take. Any rule that selects a $1-\alpha$ set gives correct coverage, so the choice, called the **ordering rule**, controls everything except validity: the length of the intervals, their behaviour at a physical boundary, and whether the procedure can return something absurd. Feldman & Cousins (1998, Ref. 18) fixed the rule; the two classical pathologies below are what they fixed.

**Pathology 1: flip-flopping.** Long-standing practice was to decide *after seeing the data* what kind of interval to quote — an upper limit if the result is not significant, a two-sided central interval if it is. That makes the ordering rule itself a function of the data, so the reported set is no longer the Neyman construction of any one rule, and its coverage is not what the label says. The left panel of the figure below measures the damage for a Gaussian measurement of $\mu \ge 0$ with the usual "upper limit below $3\sigma$, central interval above" policy: a procedure advertised as 90 % covers only **85 %** across a broad band of true $\mu$. The failure mode is undercoverage — the same disease, in the vocabulary of §6.1, that this project finds in NSBI.

**Pathology 2: empty and unphysical intervals.** With a physical boundary ($\mu \ge 0$) and a downward fluctuation — fewer events observed than the expected background — a classical central construction can return an interval lying entirely below zero: an **empty** confidence set, in which the experiment reports that no physical value of $\mu$ is allowed at 90 % confidence. Coverage is not violated (empty sets occur with exactly the right frequency), but the result is unusable, and the more sensitive the experiment, the more often it happens.

**The ordering rule.** Feldman and Cousins remove the freedom by fixing the ordering *before* the data, and fixing it with the likelihood ratio. For each hypothesis $\mu$, rank the possible outcomes $x$ by

$$
R(x)=\frac{P(x\mid\mu)}{P\big(x\mid\hat{\mu}_{\rm phys}(x)\big)}
$$

where $\hat{\mu}_{\rm phys}(x)$ is the value of $\mu$ that best fits the outcome $x$ **within the physically allowed region** — for $\mu \ge 0$, simply $\hat{\mu}_{\rm phys} = \max(\hat{\mu}, 0)$. Outcomes enter the acceptance region in order of decreasing $R$ until the accumulated probability reaches $1-\alpha$; then invert as in §6.2. Nothing else changes.

The rule is the natural one in the sense of §4: $R$ is the same likelihood ratio whose logarithm is the test statistic $t_\mu$. **Feldman–Cousins is therefore exactly the Neyman construction run with the likelihood-ratio statistic and with the thresholds taken from toys rather than from Wilks** — which is why it is the refinement named in §6.2 and in §17.6, and not a separate method.

Both pathologies then vanish, for structural reasons rather than by tuning:

- The ordering is fixed before the data, so there is no flip-flop to make: the construction itself decides where an interval stops being one-sided. The left panel shows FC coverage sitting on the nominal value everywhere.
- Every outcome lies in the acceptance region of at least one $\mu$ — its own best fit, where $R = 1$ ranks first — so an interval can never come out empty. The right panel shows the belt for a Poisson with a known background $b = 3$: the interval is the vertical slice through the belt at the observed $n$, and it passes from an upper limit at small $n$ to a two-sided interval at large $n$ smoothly, with no gap and no discontinuity. At $n = 0$ it returns $[0,\ 0.95]$; at $n = 6$, $[0.16,\ 8.46]$.

![Feldman–Cousins: flip-flopping undercovers (left); the FC belt for a Poisson with known background (right)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/feldman_cousins.png)

**The fine print.** Three limitations matter. (i) For discrete data an acceptance region cannot carry probability exactly $1-\alpha$; it overshoots, so FC intervals slightly **over**-cover. That is conservatism, not overconfidence — valid, merely a little long. (ii) FC does not cure the fact that an experiment with a strong downward fluctuation still reports a limit stronger than its sensitivity: the interval is honest about coverage while flattering the experiment, which is the objection that motivated $\mathrm{CL}_s$ in other contexts. (iii) With nuisance parameters the construction becomes expensive — toys over the whole $(\mu, \theta)$ space — and the usual compromise, the profile construction, reintroduces an approximation and with it the possibility of miscoverage.

**Why this belongs in this note.** Two conditions of §7 are precisely what the FC ordering is built for. Condition (D) is sensitivity to the boundary $\mu \ge 0$, which at 1 fb⁻¹ with ≈ 5.7 expected events is live rather than academic: a toy's scan minimum can sit at or below zero, exactly where the Wald parabola is invalid and where classical intervals misbehave. And condition (A)'s rate fold makes the model locally two-to-one in $\mu$ near the SM point, so restricting the best fit to the physical region is doing real work rather than being a formality. FC is therefore not decoration on §17.6: it is the ordering that makes the toy-calibrated fallback usable at this luminosity, and, being a Neyman construction, it carries the guarantee of §6.2 — correct coverage whether or not any asymptotic condition holds.

### 6.4 Coverage vs the Asimov check — two different questions

Both diagnostics are aimed at the same underlying disease: a test statistic whose sampling distribution is not the one the analysis assumes. They are nevertheless *not* the same measurement, and neither implies the other. The cleanest way to see why is to lay out the chain that turns data into an error bar, because the two diagnostics cut it at different points.

1. The analysis supplies a likelihood $\mathcal{L}(\mu)$ — the true one for a histogram, a learned approximation for NSBI (§7C).
2. From it comes the statistic $t_\mu = -2\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}$, which has some sampling distribution $f(t_\mu \mid \mu)$ over repeated experiments.
3. A **threshold** converts the statistic into an interval: report every $\mu$ with $t_\mu \le c$. Asymptotics assert $c = 1$ for 68 %, from the $\chi^2$ quantile that Wilks promises.
4. The interval is the set that survives.

**The Asimov check tests step 2, and only its median.** It asks whether the curve computed on the expectation-value dataset equals the pointwise median of the toy curves. That is a statement about the *median* of $f(t_\mu \mid \mu)$ as a function of $\mu$, and about nothing else. It never mentions a confidence level, never picks a threshold, and never asks where the truth ended up. Both objects being compared — the Asimov curve and the toy median — are produced by the analysis itself, under the same assumed $\mu'$.

**Coverage tests steps 3–4 against reality.** It asks whether the interval, cut at the threshold the analysis actually uses, contains the true $\mu'$ as often as advertised. That is a statement about a *quantile* of $f(t_\mu \mid \mu)$ — whether its 68th percentile really sits at $c = 1$ — confronted with a truth that the Asimov check never invokes.

So the two look at different features of the same distribution: **the Asimov check looks at its median; coverage looks at its $1-\alpha$ quantile against the assumed threshold.** A distribution can have the median it is supposed to have and the wrong 68th percentile, or the reverse. This also explains an asymmetry in what they report: coverage is level-dependent, so a procedure can cover at 68 % and miscover at 95 %, and coverage must therefore be mapped over levels as well as over $\mu'$ (§6.1); the Asimov property is a single curve comparison with no level in it at all.

**The consequence that matters most here: the Asimov check is blind to any error the Asimov and the toys share.** The Asimov dataset and every toy are analysed with the *same* likelihood — for NSBI, the same frozen $\hat{r}$ (§7C). An error that distorts both in the same direction largely cancels in the difference between them. If a learned ratio systematically over-steepens the likelihood, the Asimov curve and the toy median can both be too steep, agree beautifully with each other, and every interval cut from them still be too short. The Asimov check would pass and the analysis would be overconfident. A coverage measurement cannot be fooled this way, because it does not compare the analysis with itself — it compares it with the truth.

The reverse blindness is just as real. A toy-calibrated analysis (§6.2, §17.6) has correct coverage **by construction**, whatever the statistic does, because the thresholds are taken from the toys rather than from Wilks. Such an analysis can violate the Asimov property flagrantly and still cover exactly. Its published interval is valid; what is wrong is its *projected* sensitivity — the number quoted before the data are taken, and the one used to plan the experiment.

The four cases, and what each would mean:

| | Coverage correct | Coverage fails |
|---|---|---|
| **Asimov = median** | Asymptotics effectively hold. Nothing to fix. | Both curves are distorted *together* — a common-mode error in the shared likelihood, or a non-$\chi^2$ distribution whose median happens to be right. Expected sensitivity is quoted correctly; the error bar is not. **The Asimov check alone would have declared this analysis healthy.** |
| **Asimov ≠ median** | The thresholds were calibrated with toys (Neyman/Feldman–Cousins), so validity is bought by construction. The measurement is sound; the Asimov *prediction* of sensitivity is not. | What this project appears to observe: the toy curves are steeper than the Asimov, so intervals cut at $\Delta = 1$ are too short and the procedure under-covers (§11). |

Two practical differences follow. The Asimov check is the cheaper measurement — one Asimov fit and one toy ensemble at a single assumed $\mu'$ — while coverage needs toys at *many* true $\mu'$, an interval extracted per toy, and the whole thing repeated per confidence level. And their guarantees have different origins: coverage is what the Neyman construction delivers unconditionally (§6.2), whereas the Asimov property is guaranteed by nothing outside the Wald approximation (§5).

Neither diagnostic subsumes the other, and each is blind exactly where the other sees. Both are run.

## 7. When does the Asimov property hold? The four conditions

The folklore answer — *"you need enough events in every bin"* — is not the condition. It is a **binned proxy** for one of the conditions, in one particular representation, and that is why it fails to transport to NSBI: an NSBI likelihood has no bins, so the folklore rule has nothing to say about it, and one is tempted to conclude, wrongly, that a large *total* event count is enough. Unpacking "asymptotic" gives four separate requirements: three behind Wilks/Wald, and a fourth imposed by testing curves rather than scalars.

### (A) Regularity of the model at the truth

The classical conditions require the Fisher information to be finite, positive and **non-vanishing** at the true parameter; the truth to lie in the **interior** of the parameter space; and the parameter to be **identifiable**, the map from $\mu$ to the distribution being locally one-to-one. If the information vanishes at the truth, the likelihood is locally flatter than quadratic, $\hat{\mu}$ is not asymptotically Gaussian, and its variance does not scale like $1/N$. No amount of data repairs this: it is a property of the *model*, not the sample.

**This condition is not automatic in the present model, and the culprit is the interference.** Differentiating the total rate of §2.2:

$$
\frac{d\nu_{\rm tot}}{d\mu}=L\left(\sigma_S+\frac{\sigma_I}{2\sqrt{\mu}}\right)
$$

Because $\sigma_I$ is *negative*, this derivative has a zero: the rate is stationary — minimal — at

$$
\mu^*=\left(\frac{\sigma_I}{2\,\sigma_S}\right)^{2}\approx 0.957
$$

which lies **essentially on top of the Standard Model point being measured**. With the §2.3 cross-sections, the derivative at $\mu = 1$ is $\sigma_S+\sigma_I/2 \approx 0.0067$ fb, a factor ~46 below $\sigma_S$ itself. Two consequences follow: the event *count* carries almost no first-order information about $\mu$ near the SM, and the rate is locally **two-to-one** around $\mu^*$ — two different $\mu$ on either side of the fold predict the same total rate, which is a local identifiability failure. Figure 4 shows both.

![The interference rate fold](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/rate_fold.png)

This is a statement about the *parametrisation and the physics*, not about binning, machine learning, or sample size. It applies equally to histogram and NSBI, and, being an information deficit that data cannot fill, it becomes *more* visible as luminosity grows, not less.

### (B) A central limit theorem for the score

This is the condition the folklore approximates. The relevant object is the **score** — the derivative of the log-likelihood, a *sum over events*:

$$
U(\mu)=\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mu}=-\nu'(\mu)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln\big[\nu(\mu)\,p(x_i|\mu)\big]
$$

Asymptotic normality of $\hat{\mu}$ comes from a CLT applied to this sum. For the CLT to bite, two things must hold: **many contributing terms**, and **no small subset dominating** (formally a Lindeberg-type condition: finite per-event variance, no heavy tail in the contributions).

In each representation:

- **In a histogram**, every event in bin $b$ contributes the *same* score term $\partial_\mu\ln\nu_b(\mu)$. The sum collapses onto a handful of distinct values weighted by bin counts, and the CLT's demands reduce to "each bin's count is large enough to be Gaussian". This is the origin of "≥ 10 events per bin": a sufficient condition for (B) in one representation, not a definition of asymptotics.
- **In NSBI there are no bins.** Every event contributes its own $\partial_\mu\ln r(x_i|\mu)$ — a continuous quantity that can be arbitrarily large for an event in a sparse corner of the ≈ 9-dimensional space. The sum can be dominated by a handful of events even when $n$ looks comfortable.

The representation-independent invariant is therefore **the effective number of events in the score sum**, for instance the Kish effective sample size

$$
N_{\rm eff}=\frac{\big(\sum_i u_i\big)^2}{\sum_i u_i^2},\qquad u_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln r(x_i|\mu)
$$

which equals $n$ for equal contributions and collapses toward 1 when one event dominates. A 100-event NSBI dataset whose log-ratio sum is carried by a few events is, statistically, a few-event experiment. $N_{\rm eff}$ is the translation of "enough events per bin" into a language a per-event likelihood can hear, and it is what a dimensionality/occupancy study probes. Figure 5 shows two datasets with identical $N$ and wildly different $N_{\rm eff}$.

![Effective sample size of the score](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/neff_score.png)

### (C) Correct specification

Wilks and Wald assume the likelihood being maximised **is the true likelihood of the data**. The assumption is routinely satisfied, and correspondingly easy to overlook.

- For a **histogram** it is free: bin counts are unbiased estimators of bin expectations by construction, so the binned Poisson likelihood is the exact likelihood of the binned data. (Binning throws information away; what remains is perfectly modelled.)
- For **NSBI it is never exactly true.** The per-event ratio is *learned* from finite training data; the scanned likelihood is a *different function* from the true one. Under mis-specification, the MLE converges to a "pseudo-true" value and the curvature of the wrong likelihood is not the variance of anything.

The decisive detail is that **the estimation error is frozen into the analysis**. It is the same $\hat{r}$ for every event, every toy, every luminosity; it does not average away as data accumulate, the way statistical noise does. This is the one condition *structurally unavailable* to NSBI, and the reason no mitigation can consist of "collect more events".

### (D) Curve-level quadraticity — the extra requirement imposed here

Conditions (A)–(C) suffice for the scalar theorem of §5. The property tested here is stronger: pointwise agreement of whole scan *curves* across the window. That additionally requires the parabola to hold *over the range scanned*, not just near the minimum, and it is sensitive to the physical boundary $\mu \ge 0$ (at low luminosity the minimum can sit near or beyond it, where the Wald form is invalid). (D) is kept separate because when the curve-level test fails, no theorem of Wilks or Cowan has been violated.

### Summary table

| | Condition | Fails when… | Histogram | NSBI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Regularity at the truth | the rate is stationary in $\mu$ — as here, at $\mu^* \approx 0.957$, by destructive interference | at risk | at risk (same parametrisation) |
| B | CLT for the score | few *effective* events carry the sum — sparse cells, heavy-tailed log-ratios | buyable: widen bins | not buyable: no bin-width knob |
| C | Correct specification | the likelihood is *estimated* rather than known | free by construction | structurally violated |
| D | Quadraticity across the scan | non-parabolic curves; $\hat{\mu}$ near the $\mu = 0$ boundary | at risk at low $L$ | at risk at low $L$ |

The three mechanisms this project can plausibly find are (A), (B) and (C), and they are *distinguishable*, because they respond differently to luminosity: (A) grows with $L$, (B) shrinks with $L$, (C) is flat in $L$. That row of luminosity dependence is the experimental design contained in the theory.

## 8. Why a histogram can buy the conditions and NSBI cannot

The project's framing hypothesis follows from §7 in two steps.

A **histogram analysis** compresses each event to one observable and bins it. The analyst controls the bin width: if a bin fails condition (B), merge bins until it does not. Coarse binning discards information but *buys asymptotic validity*, and costs nothing in condition (C), because binned counts remain exactly modelled however coarse the bins. That knob is why the histogram behaves at every luminosity.

An **NSBI analysis** evaluates a per-event ratio at each event's exact point in the ≈ 9-dimensional space — the limit of an infinitely finely binned histogram. The effective "cell" around an event contains, in expectation, far less than one event; and there is **no bin-widening knob**, because giving up resolution is precisely what NSBI exists to avoid. Further, the act of replacing bins by a learned function is itself what breaks condition (C).

> 💡 **Analogy.** A histogram lets the analyst choose the bucket size; NSBI hands every event its own bucket. Buckets can always be made big enough that each holds a crowd — but a bucket sized for one occupant is never crowded, no matter how many buckets there are.

The consequence, and the hypothesis worth falsifying: *for high-dimensional per-event analyses, the non-asymptotic regime extends to far larger total event counts than one-dimensional intuition suggests.* The cleanest test takes machine learning **out of the picture entirely**: add axes to a plain histogram. If a multi-dimensional histogram starts failing the Asimov property at the cell occupancies where NSBI fails, the mechanism is condition (B) and the network is exonerated; if it does not, condition (C) is implicated. Either outcome is decisive, which is why this leads the research programme (§17.4).

## 9. Learning the likelihood ratio: CARL and NSBI, from first principles

The densities $p_S, p_B, p_{\rm SBI}$ in the full kinematic space are not known in closed form — only samples exist. Simulation-based inference solves this with a classifier, and the result rests on a theorem derived below.

### 9.1 The likelihood-ratio trick, derived

Train a classifier $s(x) \in (0,1)$ to separate samples of $p_{\rm num}$ (label $y = 1$) from samples of $p_{\rm ref}$ (label $y = 0$), mixed in equal proportions, by minimising the standard binary cross-entropy

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[-y\ln s(x)-(1-y)\ln(1-s(x))\big]
$$

Because the loss is an expectation over $x$, it can be minimised **pointwise**: at each $x$, the integrand is $-\pi_1(x)\ln s-(1-\pi_1(x))\ln(1-s)$ with $\pi_1(x)=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm num}(x)+p_{\rm ref}(x)}$ the local probability that a sample at $x$ carries label 1. Differentiating with respect to $s$ and setting to zero:

$$
-\frac{\pi_1}{s}+\frac{1-\pi_1}{1-s}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^*(x)=\pi_1(x)=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm num}(x)+p_{\rm ref}(x)}
$$

Rearranged, the *optimally trained* classifier encodes the density ratio exactly:

$$
\frac{s^*(x)}{1-s^*(x)}=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm ref}(x)}
$$

A *discriminative* network has produced a *density ratio*, with no density estimation anywhere. This is CARL (Cranmer–Pavez–Louppe). The derivation assumes infinite data, unlimited capacity and exact optimisation. A real network satisfies none of the three, which is why CARL's second ingredient — **calibration**, diagnosing and correcting the residual distortion of $\hat{s}/(1-\hat{s})$ — is part of the method's name rather than an optional extra. It is also where condition (C) of §7 enters: the gap between $s^*$ and $\hat{s}$ *is* the mis-specification.

### 9.2 The NSBI construction used here

Choose the reference $p_{\rm ref}=p_B$ and train **two** CARL networks: one for $p_S/p_B$, one for $p_{\rm SBI}/p_B$. Feeding both into the mixture formula of §2.2 gives the full $\mu$-dependent ratio for *any* $\mu$:

$$
\frac{p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)}{p_B(x)}=\frac{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S\,\frac{p_S}{p_B}(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}\,\frac{p_{\rm SBI}}{p_B}(x)+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}
$$

The arbitrary reference cancels in ratios of ratios, so its choice is harmless; and the $\mu$-dependence lives entirely in *known coefficients*, so the networks are trained **once** — on balanced, shape-only samples carrying **no luminosity information** — and every hypothesis is evaluated by re-mixing the same two outputs. Luminosity enters only at inference. This is the origin of hypothesis 3 in §12.

![The CARL/NSBI pipeline](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/carl_pipeline.png)

## 10. The two likelihoods, side by side

Both analyses are extended-likelihood fits of the same $\mu$.

**Histogram** — ten log-spaced $m_{4\ell}$ bins:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\prod_{\rm bins}\mathcal{P}\big(n_b\mid\nu_b(\mu)\big)
$$

The product of per-bin Poissons automatically contains the total-rate information, since the bin expectations sum to the total. Nothing needs adding.

**NSBI** — a per-event shape product times one global rate term:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\mathcal{P}\big(n\mid\nu(\mu)\big)\prod_{\rm events}\frac{p_{\rm SBI}(x_i|\mu)}{p_{\rm ref}(x_i)},\qquad\nu(\mu)=L\big(\mu\,\sigma_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\sigma_I+\sigma_B\big)
$$

Two structural remarks. Dividing the shape product by $p_{\rm ref}$ is harmless: it is $\mu$-independent and cancels in $-2\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}$. And **the rate term is analytic** — computed from known cross-sections, exact, never touched by a network. The networks supply shape information and nothing else.

> 🎯 **The rate term is where the luminosity dependence lives.** The shape term gains information per event; the rate term gains information as $\nu(\mu)$ grows with $L$, and it is exact. As luminosity increases, an exactly-known, well-behaved term takes over from an approximate one — the natural explanation for why an NSBI analysis would agree with its Asimov at very high luminosity and disagree below. Corollary: an NSBI likelihood *without* its rate term is pure shape, hence pure neural network, and inherits every pathology of the network with nothing to dilute it.
>
> Note the tension with §7(A): near the SM point the rate term of *this particular model* is nearly flat in $\mu$, so "the rate term takes over" cannot be the whole story close to $\mu = 1$. It constrains the far tails, where $\nu(\mu)$ finally varies, more than the neighbourhood of the truth.

## 11. The founding observation

Schematically (Figure 7 — a redrawn cartoon, not measured curves): in the **histogram** analysis the median of the toy scans tracks the Asimov scan at every luminosity, band and all. In the **NSBI** analysis at low luminosity the toy median is systematically **steeper** than the Asimov — each pseudo-experiment claims *more* sensitivity than the expectation-level fit predicts — with the discrepancy shrinking as luminosity grows and vanishing when the rate term dominates.

![The founding observation, schematic](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/asimov_vs_toys_schematic.png)

The direction fixes the vocabulary: the *toys* are the steeper curves, so each toy analysis is **overconfident** relative to the Asimov expectation, and the Asimov *understates* what the toys claim. In coverage language (§6): if the toy-level curvature is too large, the $\Delta = 1$ intervals cut from toy curves are too short, and the procedure under-covers. The contrast is controlled — same events, same toy recipe, same comparator, with only the analysis method changing between the two panels — which is what makes the observation worth explaining rather than dismissing.

## 12. Why could NSBI be overconfident? Hypotheses

Four candidate mechanisms, not mutually exclusive, each mapped to a condition of §7, which is what makes them separable experiments.

1. **NN mis-specification** *(condition C)*. The learned ratio deviates from the true one; a wrong likelihood is being scanned, and no theorem governs the curvature of a wrong likelihood. The observation that agreement improves exactly when the exact rate term dominates points at the NN-supplied piece.
2. **No unbiased NN estimator exists** *(condition C, sharpened)*. Bin counts are unbiased estimators of bin expectations by construction; a trained network is an unbiased estimator of nothing, ensembled or not. Some bias in the learned log-ratio is unavoidable and its effect on curvature is uncontrolled.
3. **Training is blind to sample-size fluctuations** *(condition B, seen from the estimator's side)*. The networks are trained once, on a single large balanced sample, with no luminosity information (§9.2). The learned ratio is a statement about *densities*; nothing in the training objective represents what a six-event dataset does to a shape estimate. In the team's phrasing, the network *"doesn't realise how limited it is by the statistics of the dataset"* — it has never been shown a small dataset.
4. **SBI is generically overconfident** *(the external prior)*. The ML literature has documented, across SBI algorithm families including CARL's, that approximate posteriors are systematically less conservative than the truth unless explicitly diagnosed and corrected (§13).

> 💡 Hypotheses 1–2 say the network is **wrong**; hypothesis 3 bites even for a *perfectly* learned ratio, because it concerns what a finite dataset does to the statistic's sampling distribution, not the quality of the density estimate. Disentangling them is what the research programme is for, and §7's luminosity-dependence row is the instrument.

## 13. What the SBI literature already knows: overconfidence, ensembles, and balanced training

### 13.1 The trust-crisis result

The project's frequentist observation has a Bayesian twin, documented at benchmark scale. Hermans et al. (Ref. 5) present extensive empirical evidence that simulation-based inference algorithms "can produce computationally unfaithful posterior approximations", and the finding is family-wide — verbatim: "all benchmarked algorithms – (Sequential) Neural Posterior Estimation, (Sequential) Neural Ratio Estimation, Sequential Neural Likelihood and variants of Approximate Bayesian Computation – can yield overconfident posterior approximations, which makes them unreliable for scientific use cases". Neural ratio estimation — CARL's family — is on that list explicitly. This is not an anecdote about one badly tuned network; it is systematic behaviour of the method class across simulators and budgets, worst at small ones.

**Their diagnostic is coverage, in exactly §6's sense.** For a posterior approximation $\hat{p}(\vartheta|x)$ and credible level $1-\alpha$, let $\Theta(1-\alpha)$ be the highest-posterior-density region integrating to $1-\alpha$; the **expected coverage probability**

$$
\mathrm{ECP}(1-\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta,x)}\Big[\mathbb{1}\big(\vartheta\in\Theta_{\hat{p}(\vartheta\mid x)}(1-\alpha)\big)\Big]
$$

is measured by simulating pairs $(\vartheta^*, x)$ from the joint and asking how often the truth lands in the claimed region. Faithful: ECP $= 1-\alpha$ at every level. Conservative: $\ge$. **Overconfident: $<$** — and the BNRE paper notes that this quantity "can be interpreted as the expected frequentist coverage": prior-averaged over $\vartheta^*$, it *is* §6's coverage. The Bayesian and frequentist literatures are diagnosing the same disease with the same instrument.

### 13.2 Ensembles — what they fix and what they cannot

The mitigation Hermans et al. themselves report is **ensembling**: train $M$ networks that differ only in initialisation (and possibly data shuffling), and combine their outputs. The canonical reference is Deep Ensembles (Ref. 16). Training a network is itself a noisy algorithm — random initialisation, stochastic optimisation, one finite training sample — so the learned $\hat{r}$ is one draw from a distribution over functions. The ensemble spread estimates that **model/training uncertainty**, and averaging over members smooths out the individual networks' idiosyncratic errors: a variance reduction on the estimator, hence more faithful (less overconfident) inference on average.

Ensembles, toys, and the conditions of §7 are three different objects, and the distinction is the most commonly confused point in the subject:

- **Toys** fluctuate the *data*; they estimate statistical uncertainty at fixed analysis. (Condition B territory.)
- **Ensembles** fluctuate the *training*; they estimate the estimator's variance at fixed data distribution. (The variance half of condition C.)
- **Neither** addresses the estimator's *bias* — the part of condition C that averaging over ensemble members cannot remove, because every member is trained toward the same wrong-at-finite-capacity optimum. And neither teaches the analysis that a six-event dataset deserves humility, so hypothesis 3 stands untouched by both.

As put in the April meeting: *ensembling would be the same move as the toys but for a different uncertainty — toys cover the statistics of the data, ensembling catches the bias/variance of the network* — together with the sharper observation that an **un**-optimised single network makes the low-luminosity overconfidence *easier* to see, because tuning the network attacks neither the sample-size blindness nor the frozen bias.

### 13.3 Balanced NRE — buying conservatism in the objective

The sharper structural fix in the literature is **Balanced Neural Ratio Estimation** (BNRE, Ref. 13). In NRE, a classifier $\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)$ separates joint samples $p(\vartheta,x)$ from product-of-marginals samples $p(\vartheta)p(x)$; the Bayes-optimal classifier encodes the likelihood-to-evidence ratio (same derivation as §9.1, different pair of densities). BNRE restricts the hypothesis space to **balanced** classifiers, those satisfying

$$
\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta,x)}\big[\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta)p(x)}\big[\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)\big]=1
$$

Three theorems make the restriction attractive: every balanced classifier is, *in expectation*, less confident than the Bayes-optimal one; and the Bayes-optimal classifier is itself balanced — so the restriction costs nothing at convergence, giving the same optimum with a conservative tendency at finite budget. It is implemented as one penalty term added to the classification loss, with no extra networks and no extra compute.

> 💡 **Why this matters operationally here.** The two CARL networks are binary classifiers of exactly this type. A BNRE-style balancing penalty is a drop-in modification of the CARL training loss — one line — which makes "conservative training objectives" a cheap experiment rather than a literature pointer, and gives every other mitigation a conservative baseline to beat (§17.5).

## 14. The Bartlett correction, from first principles

### 14.1 The idea

In finite samples $t_\mu$ is not $\chi^2$-distributed. The lowest-order symptom sits in its **mean**: a $\chi^2$ with one degree of freedom has mean exactly 1, but a systematic expansion of the LR statistic in powers of $1/n$ ($n$ = sample size) gives

$$
\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]=1+\frac{b}{n}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
$$

with $b$ a model-dependent constant. Bartlett's correction (1937) rescales the statistic by its actual mean:

$$
t'_\mu=\frac{t_\mu}{\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]}
$$

so that $\mathbb{E}[t'_\mu]=1$ **by construction**. The non-trivial content — due to Lawley — is that fixing the first moment repairs the *entire distribution* at the next order: for regular parametric families, the $\chi^2$ approximation error of the corrected statistic improves from $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$ to $\mathcal{O}(1/n^2)$. Every quantile, not only the mean, moves toward $\chi^2$. That across-the-board improvement is what earns the name "correction" rather than "normalisation": a single scalar, measured or computed, buys an order in $1/n$.

### 14.2 Obtaining the scalar

There are two routes to $\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]$. **Analytic**, where the model permits an expansion — Cowan (2019, Ref. 9) works a HEP example (his gamma-variance/error-on-error model, $\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]=1+3r^2+cr^4$) and notes the method "has received some limited notice in Particle Physics". **Monte Carlo**, in his words: "one could in principle find the expectation value E[t_μ] by the Monte Carlo method". For an NN-defined statistic no analytic expansion exists, so the MC route is the only one — and it is nearly free here, because a toy ensemble already produces, at every $(\mu, L)$, the full empirical distribution of $t_\mu$; its **mean** is a by-product of scans that are run anyway. Note the division of labour: Bartlett needs the *mean*, while the Asimov comparator uses the *median*. Same ensemble, two different summaries.

### 14.3 Conditions of validity

Bartlett's guarantee presumes conditions (A) and (C) of §7: a *regular*, *correctly specified* model. Where the information is nearly stationary (the fold of §7A) the regular-family theorems do not apply — an empirical mean-rescaling may still help at a given quantile, but it arrives without its warranty. And no rescaling of a statistic repairs the statistic's *model* being wrong.

> 🎯 That limitation is what makes the correction **diagnostic**: *if a Bartlett-type rescaling reconciles Asimov and toys, the failure was distributional — condition (B) territory; if it does not, the estimator itself is implicated — condition (C).* One extra curve per panel, no new training, and it separates the two leading hypotheses of §12.

## 15. MC statistical uncertainties: the Barlow–Beeston idea

There is a second finite sample hiding in every template analysis: the **simulation**. The bin expectations $\nu_b(\mu)$ (or the learned ratio) are *estimated* from finite MC, so they carry statistical errors of their own, and a likelihood that treats them as exact is overconfident.

**The full treatment** (Barlow & Beeston 1993, Ref. 14): promote each bin's true MC expectation to a nuisance parameter, constrained by the observed MC count as an auxiliary Poisson measurement, and profile. The likelihood then *knows* the templates are uncertain: bins with poor MC support contribute less information, intervals widen accordingly, and the widening is largest exactly where occupancy is worst — which ties this directly to condition (B).

**The "lite" simplification**, standard in modern HEP fits: one multiplicative nuisance $\gamma_b$ per bin (rather than per bin per source), constrained by the bin's **effective MC sample size**

$$
n_{\rm eff}=\frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}
$$

— the same Kish formula as §7(B), now applied to weights rather than score terms; for weighted MC it is the honest answer to "how many events is this bin really based on". The per-bin profile has a closed form, so the machinery adds no minimiser and negligible cost.

**Relevance here.** It is the conservative correction that attacks the *simulation-side* part of condition (C) — the one piece of "the likelihood is not exact" that has a classical, guaranteed-valid fix. It will not repair a learned ratio's bias, but it removes one alibi: any overconfidence that survives MC-stat profiling cannot be blamed on finite simulation.

### 15.1 The network-side counterpart: $w_i f_i$ ensembles (the April meeting's method, identified 2026-07-13)

Barlow–Beeston answers "the *templates* were estimated from finite MC". Its network-side counterpart answers "the *learned ratio* was estimated from finite data", and the April meeting's *"new Wi-Fi ensemble method"*, with which MC-stat uncertainties are *"trivial to apply"*, is exactly that. The transcription was never garbled: the method is **$w_i f_i$ ensembles** (Ref. 17, Benevedes & Thaler 2025), a pun on weights $w_i$ times basis functions $f_i$.

**The construction.** Train $M$ networks $f_i(x)$ (bootstrap resamples, or a partition of the training set), then **freeze them** and treat them as a *basis* rather than as $M$ competing answers. Model the log density ratio as a linear combination, $\log \tilde r(x|w) = \sum_i w_i f_i(x)$, and fit only the $M$ weights on a **held-out** sample with a convex (symmetrised MLC) loss. Because the loss is linear in $w$, everything downstream is closed-form: a sandwich covariance $C = V^{-1} U V^{-1}$ ($V$ = Hessian of the loss, $U$ = score covariance) gives a pointwise uncertainty on the learned log-ratio, $\sigma^2(x) = f_i(x)\, C_{ij}\, f_j(x)$, which propagates to the POI by the Gong–Samaniego pseudo-MLE result $\sigma^2_{\rm GS} = \sigma^2_{\rm MLE}\,(1 + \sigma^2_{\rm MLE} A_i C_{ij} A_j)$. That is the sense in which it is *trivial to apply*: there is **no bootstrap loop over the entire analysis**, and the uncertainty arrives as an analytic *inflation* of the usual MLE variance.

**Against §13.2.** Plain deep ensembling (Ref. 16) averages $M$ networks with uniform weights $1/M$ and calls their spread the model uncertainty. Benevedes & Thaler show that this **naive uniform-weight ensemble undercovers and is biased**, while *fitting* the weights and carrying their covariance reaches nominal coverage ($M = 32$, quark/gluon jets). Ensembling-as-averaging is not a calibration fix; ensembling-as-a-fitted-basis, with the fit's covariance propagated, can be.

**Two boundaries on its applicability here.** (i) *Scope:* the paper is explicit that $C \sim O(1/N_{\rm fit})$ captures the noise of the finite **fitting** sample — **not** mismodelling from limited network expressivity, training stochasticity, finite *training* data, or simulator systematics. It closes one term of condition (C), not the condition. (ii) *Regime:* every guarantee is **asymptotic** (fixed $M$, $N_{\rm fit} \to \infty$; Gong–Samaniego is a delta-method inflation of $\sigma^2_{\rm MLE}$, which presumes the Wald picture of §7 in the first place). This project exists because that picture fails at $O(100)$ events. The method is therefore a **candidate mitigation to be tested, not a fix to be assumed** — and the test is itself the interesting experiment: what does a method whose uncertainties are derived asymptotically do in the regime where asymptotics break? It inflates variances, so it pushes in the right direction against overconfidence; whether it inflates them *enough*, and at the right places in $\mu$, is an empirical question this project is equipped to ask (§17.3).

## 16. Deep Sets and toy-ensemble training

### 16.1 The mechanism to attack

Hypothesis 3 of §12: standard CARL training sees one large balanced sample and learns a statement about *densities*; nothing in training encodes what a six-event dataset does to a ratio estimate. The proposed cure is to train on **many pseudo-experiments**, so the network's output can depend on the dataset *as a whole*, including how big it is. That requires a network whose input is a *dataset* — an unordered, variable-size collection.

### 16.2 Why sets need a theorem

The hypothesis class must be permutation-invariant **by construction**, not by hoping augmentation teaches it. Deep Sets (Ref. 12) characterises the class exactly: a function $f(X)$ on sets (from a countable universe) is permutation-invariant **iff** it decomposes as

$$
f(X)=\rho\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\phi(x)\Big)
$$

**Proof sketch.** ($\Leftarrow$) A sum is blind to the order of its terms, and $\rho$ of a permutation-invariant quantity is permutation-invariant. ($\Rightarrow$) Choose $\phi$ to be an *injective multiset encoder*: for a countable universe one can send the $k$-th element to digits in a positional code, e.g. $\phi(x_k) = 4^{-k}$, so that the **sum alone already determines the entire multiset** — which elements, with which multiplicities. Then let $\rho$ be "decode the sum, apply $f$". Any permutation-invariant $f$ is therefore of the form $\rho(\Sigma\phi)$. In practice $\phi$ and $\rho$ are learned networks (universal approximators standing in for encoder and decoder). The paper's fine print — for *uncountable* domains such as continuous kinematics the decomposition is proven for fixed-size sets and conjectured in general — changes nothing operationally.

### 16.3 The load-bearing detail: sum, not mean

With **sum** pooling, adding events *moves* the pooled vector, so $\rho$ can read multiplicity as well as shape and learned confidence *can* scale with $N$. With **mean** pooling the same network sees only shape and is structurally blind to sample size — the precise blindness being cured. (For weighted events, two natural encodings: feed $\phi$ the pair (kinematics, toy weight), or realise weights as multiplicities.) This design choice is the connection between the architecture and condition (B).

![Deep Sets architecture](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/deepsets.png)

### 16.4 Design space, and the honest caveat

Candidate outputs, in increasing ambition: (a) a per-dataset *correction* to the standard NSBI curve — learn only the finite-$N$ distortion, safest, anchored to the existing analysis; (b) an amortised per-dataset likelihood-ratio *curve* over the $\mu$ grid; (c) the interval or statistic directly. Two open issues: generalisation across luminosities (train at several $L$, test between), and the set network's **own calibration** — it is a trained estimator too, so condition (C) and §13's diagnostics apply to it *recursively*.

> 💡 **What this does and does not fix.** A set network that has *seen* six-event datasets can learn how much uncertainty six events imply — condition (B), from the estimator's side. It does **not** make its own learned mapping correct — condition (C) — which is why the natural end-state is a *combination*: a learned, sample-size-aware summary statistic, **calibrated classically with toys** via the Neyman construction of §6.2. The learning buys power; the construction buys validity. Neither alone buys both.

## 17. The research programme

Each direction gives what it is, why it should work, how it would be done here, and what each outcome would mean. Every direction attacks or diagnoses a named condition of §7.

### 17.1 Toy-ensemble training with a set architecture — attacks (B) via the estimator

**What.** Train a permutation-invariant network (§16) on unlimited toy pseudo-experiments (§3 provides a generator with $\mu$ and $L$ varied per sample), so the analysis itself becomes a function of the whole dataset, sample size included.
**Why it should work.** It supplies, at training time, exactly the information whose absence defines hypothesis 3: the look of finite datasets. The sum-pooled representation can carry $N$, so the learned uncertainty can scale with the available statistics — which single-Asimov training structurally cannot.
**How.** The generator draws $(\mu_{\rm true}, L)$, throws one toy per sample, and feeds the padded/masked event set; outputs per §16.4, starting at ambition (a) or a simple $(\hat{\mu}, \sigma)$ regression; evaluate coverage across the $(\mu_{\rm true}, L)$ plane.
**Outcomes.** Learned uncertainty tracking the toy scatter across $L$ means mechanism (B) is learnable, and the failure was sample-size blindness. Residual miscoverage after calibration is the estimator's own condition-(C) share, isolated and measurable.

### 17.2 Corrected test statistics: Bartlett and beyond — corrects (B) at the statistic level

**What.** Rescale $t_\mu$ by its toy-ensemble mean, per hypothesis (§14); compare Asimov vs toys on corrected curves.
**Why.** The cheapest correction on the table: one scalar per $(\mu, L)$, already produced by scans that are run anyway; theory promises an order in $1/n$ where the model is regular.
**How.** Per-hypothesis means from the existing ensembles; corrected curves; re-ask the Asimov question; check the level-dependence of what the correction achieves.
**Outcomes.** As in §14.3: rescaling reconciles Asimov and toys → distributional failure, (B); it does not → estimator implicated, (C). Both outcomes are informative.

### 17.3 MC statistical uncertainties — closes the simulation half of (C)

**What.** Barlow–Beeston(-lite) profiling in both analyses (§15).
**Why.** Conservative by construction; it removes the "the templates were finite" alibi from every other comparison, and the widening concentrates exactly where occupancy is worst, so it doubles as an occupancy probe.
**How.** Per-bin $\gamma$ with closed-form profile and $n_{\rm eff}$ constraints in the histogram family; for NSBI, template-side profiling of the rate/shape inputs, plus the **network side via $w_i f_i$ ensembles** (§15.1, Ref. 17): $M$ frozen basis networks, weights fitted on a held-out sample, the analytic weight covariance propagated to $\mu$ by Gong–Samaniego. The April meeting's method is identified (2026-07-13) and is no longer a blocker on this direction; what it needs is a *test*, not a confirmation.
**The sharp version of the test.** The $w_i f_i$ uncertainties are themselves asymptotic (fixed $M$, $N_{\rm fit} \to \infty$), so applying them at 1–10 fb⁻¹ asks whether an asymptotically-derived inflation can repair a failure *caused by* the breakdown of asymptotics. Both outcomes are publishable: it restores coverage → the missing ingredient was ratio-estimation noise, and the cure is cheap; it does not → the overconfidence is not fitting-sample noise at all, and hypothesis 3 (sample-size blindness, §17.1) survives a serious challenge from the one method the field would reach for first.
**Outcomes.** If miscoverage persists with MC-stat profiled, finite simulation is exonerated and the remaining suspects are (A)/(B)/(C-network). If it vanishes, the entire story was template statistics — the cheapest possible resolution.

### 17.4 The dimensionality study — separates (B) from (C) with no ML in sight

**What.** Add axes to a plain histogram (2D, 3D, 4D…) and scan luminosity, tracking the Asimov property as a function of cell occupancy.
**Why.** It is the decisive experiment of §8: a histogram has no learned component, so any breakdown it shows is pure condition (B). Matching NSBI's failure occupancies exonerates the network; failing to break implicates it.
**How.** Multi-dimensional binning of the same events, same toys, same comparator; report against mean and *effective* occupancy (the $N_{\rm eff}$ of §7(B), because cell population is wildly non-uniform); control at fixed total bin count to separate "dimension" from "cells".
**Outcomes.** Collapse of the mismatch onto occupancy → (B) is the mechanism, and mitigations should target statistics rather than networks. No mismatch even at NSBI-like occupancies → the learned estimator is the difference, (C). The direction of any mismatch is a falsification lever: an Asimov-*narrower* mismatch would contradict the founding observation's direction and force a rethink.

### 17.5 Conservative training objectives (BNRE) — attacks (C) in the loss

**What.** Add the balancing penalty of §13.3 to the CARL loss; everything else unchanged.
**Why.** It is the only mitigation that changes *what the network converges to*: provably conservative-in-expectation at finite budget, at zero architectural cost, and the natural baseline every fancier fix must beat.
**How.** One penalty term; scan its coefficient; evaluate with the same Asimov-vs-toys and coverage protocol.
**Outcomes.** If balance alone restores the Asimov property, the failure was classifier overconfidence in the (C) sense. If curves become *wider but still mismatched*, then conservatism ≠ calibration — an important distinction to demonstrate.

### 17.6 Toy-calibrated inference: the Neyman fallback — restores validity under all conditions

**What.** Stop taking the asymptotic shortcut entirely: per hypothesis, calibrate the statistic's threshold with toys (§6.2), whatever the statistic — the classical construction wrapped around a modern statistic.
**Why.** It is the *only* item on this list whose validity does not depend on (A), (B), (C) or (D): coverage is guaranteed by construction. The price is per-hypothesis toys and, with a poor statistic, longer intervals — validity is bought, efficiency still has to be earned.
**How.** Belts from per-hypothesis toy quantiles of any chosen statistic (the NSBI $t_\mu$, a learned summary, anything); intervals by inversion; efficiency compared across statistics; the Feldman–Cousins ordering of §6.3 as the refinement — which also supplies the boundary behaviour condition (D) demands at 1 fb⁻¹.
**Outcomes.** This is less an experiment than the safety net under all of them — and combined with 17.1 it is the natural end-state sketched in §16.4: learned power, constructed validity.

### 17.7 The effective-sample-size diagnostic — measures (B) directly

**What.** Compute the effective sample size $N_{\rm eff}$ of the score sum (§7(B)) per analysis, per luminosity, per toy.
**Why.** It is the representation-independent replacement for "events per bin": a single number that should *predict* where the Asimov property fails, if (B) is the mechanism.
**How.** Per-event score contributions from the existing machinery; Kish formula; plot breakdown metrics against $N_{\rm eff}$ across analyses and dimensions — histogram and NSBI on the *same axis*, which no occupancy metric can offer.
**Outcomes.** If curves from different analyses collapse on $N_{\rm eff}$, the project has found its universal control variable, and a publishable, transferable diagnostic that any analysis can compute for itself.

## 18. Open questions

Conceptual questions this note leaves open (operational ones live in the env-setup note; result-driven ones in the Logbook):

- **Which "median of toys" is the right comparator?** §3.5 lists three inequivalent definitions; quantitative statements are not comparable across them. Which one's failure is *interesting*, and which is a convention artefact, must be settled and stated.
- **What scalar metric should quantify "Asimov-property violation"?** Curve distance, interval-width ratio, coverage error at fixed level? The project promises a breakdown metric as a function of luminosity and dimensionality; the metric itself is an unjustified choice so far.
- **How do (A), (B), (C) separate empirically?** They respond differently to luminosity — (A) worsens, (B) improves, (C) is flat. Designing the cleanest version of that separation experiment is open.
- **Where does the histogram itself break?** At what effective occupancy does a plain histogram start violating the property? This empirically locates the asymptotic boundary in a system with no network in it.
- **Can the Asimov check and expected coverage be related quantitatively?** §13.1 supplies the dictionary (ECP = prior-averaged frequentist coverage); a pooling protocol that lets both literatures' evidence bear on one number is missing.
- **Which generator produced the samples?** Not established by any file, comment, or slide; to be confirmed with Tei, and probably prerequisite to explaining the cross-section prefactors in the dataset normalisation.

The question it *becomes*: **does an asymptotically-derived uncertainty inflation restore coverage in a regime defined by the failure of asymptotics?** (§17.3.)

## 19. Glossary

| Term | One line |
|------|----------|
| Asimov dataset | Artificial dataset in which every observable equals its expectation; fitting it returns the true parameters exactly. |
| Asimov property (operational, this project) | Asimov $-2\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}$ scan curve = pointwise-per-$\mu$ median of toy pseudo-experiment scan curves. |
| Asymptotic regime | Sample sizes and model conditions such that Wilks/Wald hold: LR statistic $\sim \chi^2$, scan curves parabolic (see §7). |
| Weighted MC sample | Simulated events with weights summing to the expected yield; dense in shape, normalised in rate. |
| Toy pseudo-experiment | One Poisson draw per event weight, $w^{\rm toy}\sim\mathcal{P}(wL)$ — an exact sample from the extended likelihood (§3.3). |
| Coverage | Fraction of repeated experiments whose reported interval contains the truth; the operational meaning of an error bar (§6). |
| Overconfidence | Coverage below the stated confidence level; intervals shorter than warranted. |
| Neyman construction | Per-hypothesis acceptance regions from toy quantiles; coverage guaranteed by construction, no asymptotics (§6.2). |
| Ordering rule | The freedom left open by the Neyman construction: *which* outcomes of probability $1-\alpha$ enter each acceptance region. Fixes efficiency and boundary behaviour, never validity (§6.3). |
| Feldman–Cousins | The ordering rule that ranks outcomes by the likelihood ratio $P(x\mid\mu)/P(x\mid\hat{\mu}_{\rm phys})$ with the best fit restricted to the physical region; cures flip-flopping and empty intervals, never over-confident (§6.3). |
| Flip-flopping | Deciding after seeing the data whether to quote an upper limit or a two-sided interval; makes the ordering data-dependent and undercovers — 85 % for a nominal 90 % (§6.3). |
| Score | Derivative of the log-likelihood in the POI; a sum over events whose Gaussianity the CLT must deliver (§7B). |
| Effective sample size $N_{\rm eff}$ | $(\Sigma u)^2/\Sigma u^2$; the representation-independent version of "events per bin". |
| Regularity (condition A) | Non-vanishing Fisher information at the truth, identifiability, interior truth. |
| Mis-specification (condition C) | The maximised likelihood is not the true likelihood — automatic for a learned ratio, impossible for binned counts. |
| Rate fold, $\mu^*$ | The stationary point of $\nu(\mu)$ at $(\sigma_I/2\sigma_S)^2$; near it the count carries no first-order $\mu$-information (§7A). |
| Extended likelihood | Poisson of the total count × product of per-event densities. |
| Rate / shape term | The Poisson factor (analytic, exact here) / the per-event product (all the networks supply). |
| POI, $\mu$ | Signal-strength modifier: amplitude × $\sqrt{\mu}$, signal rate × $\mu$; $\mu = 1$ is the SM. |
| SIG / BKG / INT / SBI | Signal, background, (destructive) interference, and their physical sum. |
| SBI / NSBI | (Neural) simulation-based inference: inference when the likelihood is only accessible through simulation. |
| CARL | Likelihood-ratio estimation via calibrated classifiers: $s/(1-s)=p_{\rm num}/p_{\rm ref}$, derived in §9.1. |
| Calibration (CARL sense) | Diagnosing/correcting the residual distortion of an imperfectly trained classifier's ratio. |
| Ensembling | Averaging networks differing in initialisation/training noise; estimates model variance, not data statistics (§13.2). |
| BNRE | Balanced neural ratio estimation: a loss constraint making learned ratios conservative in expectation (§13.3). |
| Bartlett correction | Rescaling the LR statistic by its finite-sample mean; whole distribution approaches $\chi^2$ an order faster, for regular models (§14). |
| Barlow–Beeston (lite) | Per-bin nuisance parameters for finite-MC template uncertainty, constrained by $n_{\rm eff}$ (§15). |
| $w_i f_i$ ensembles | Frozen networks as a fitted basis; the weight covariance gives an analytic uncertainty on the learned log-ratio (§15.1). |
| Deep Sets | Permutation-invariant architecture $\rho(\Sigma\phi)$ — the unique general form (§16.2). |
| Sum vs mean pooling | Sum carries multiplicity (sample size visible); mean is shape-only (§16.3). |
