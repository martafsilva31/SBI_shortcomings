## 1. The question this project asks

Before an experiment runs, physicists want to know what sensitivity to *expect*. The gold-standard answer is brute force: simulate many pseudo-experiments ("toys"), analyse each exactly as real data would be analysed, and report the median of the resulting measures — median significance, median limit, median likelihood-scan width. The convenient answer is to analyse a single artificial dataset in which every observable is set exactly to its expectation value — the **Asimov dataset** — and read the same measures off that one fit. The entire convenience rests on a claimed equivalence: *the Asimov result equals the median of the toys.*

This project asks whether that equivalence survives when the analysis is a **neural simulation-based inference (NSBI)** analysis in a low-statistics regime. The operational form of the claim tested here — the project's working definition of the **Asimov property** — is:

> the −2Δln L scan curve computed on the Asimov dataset equals, pointwise in μ, the median of the scan curves of an ensemble of toy pseudo-experiments.

The founding observation (Tei's demonstration, first team meeting) is qualitative and simple to state: a **histogram** analysis of off-shell Higgs production satisfies this property at every luminosity tried, while an **NSBI** analysis of *the same events* visibly violates it at low luminosity — the toy median comes out systematically *steeper* than the Asimov curve — with the two converging only at very high luminosity, where the exactly-known Poisson rate term dominates the likelihood. Same events, same toy recipe, same comparator; the only thing that changes is the analysis method. §11 shows the behaviour schematically; everything in between builds the machinery needed to understand why it could happen, and why it is plausible that it *must*.

Two more framing remarks, both from the team's own discussion of the result, because they set the stakes. First, the failure is one of **confidence, not of bias**: the NSBI fit does not necessarily land on a wrong μ — it lands *somewhere reasonable while claiming a precision it does not have*. That is arguably worse than a bias, because nothing in the fit output warns you. Second, the problem is not really about luminosity: **any rare process is a low-statistics process**, whatever the integrated luminosity — a 10 fb⁻¹ analysis of a rare signal is statistically a 1 fb⁻¹ analysis of a common one. Whatever breaks here breaks for every analysis whose *signal* sample is small — which is precisely the regime NSBI is advertised for.

## 2. The physics stage and the samples

### 2.1 The process

The test case is off-shell Higgs production at parton level:

$$gg \to H^* \to ZZ \to 4\ell$$

Two amplitudes lead to the same four-lepton final state: the Higgs-mediated one (a quark loop producing an off-shell Higgs which decays to a Z pair) and the continuum background (a quark box producing the Z pair directly). Both are **loop-induced** — there is no tree-level gg → ZZ — so they are of comparable size and they **interfere**. Above the 2m_Z threshold the Higgs is far off shell, the signal amplitude is small, and the interference with the box is **destructive**.

The parameter of interest is the signal-strength modifier μ, defined so that μ = 1 is the Standard Model. It scales the signal *amplitude* by $\sqrt{\mu}$; the signal-squared term therefore scales as μ and the signal–background cross term — the interference — as $\sqrt{\mu}$. Hold on to that square root: it is the origin of nearly everything peculiar about the statistics of this model (§7).

### 2.2 The probability model

Write $\nu_S, \nu_I, \nu_B$ for the component yields and $p_S, p_I, p_B$ for the component densities (each density normalised to 1; the interference "density" is signed, with $\nu_I < 0$). The per-event density under the signal+background+interference (SBI) hypothesis is the yield-weighted mixture:

$$p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)=\frac{\mu\,\nu_S\,p_S(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_I\,p_I(x)+\nu_B\,p_B(x)}{\mu\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_I+\nu_B}$$

Because a signed density is awkward for anything that wants to sample from it, the code uses an algebraically equivalent re-arrangement in terms of the *physical* SM mixture $p_{\rm SBI} \equiv p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu{=}1)$, which avoids negative weights:

$$p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)=\frac{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S\,p_S(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}\,p_{\rm SBI}(x)+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B\,p_B(x)}{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}$$

(Check the equivalence yourself: expand $\nu_{\rm SBI}\,p_{\rm SBI} = \nu_S p_S + \nu_I p_I + \nu_B p_B$ in the numerator and collect terms — the two forms are identical.) The corresponding **total rate** — the quantity the Poisson term of §10 needs — is

$$\nu_{\rm tot}(\mu)=L\left(\mu\,\sigma_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\sigma_I+\sigma_B\right)$$

with L the integrated luminosity and σ the component cross-sections.

### 2.3 Yields: the numbers that set the regime

Expected yields at μ = 1 (from [context.md](../context.md); the 1 fb⁻¹ column *is* the cross-section in fb):

| Component | 1 fb⁻¹ | 10 fb⁻¹ | 100 fb⁻¹ | 1000 fb⁻¹ |
|-----------|--------|---------|----------|-----------|
| SIG (σ_S) | 0.310  | 3.099   | 30.990   | 309.897   |
| BKG (σ_B) | 5.953  | 59.530  | 595.303  | 5953.034  |
| INT (σ_I) | −0.606 | −6.064  | −60.643  | −606.433  |
| SBI total | 5.656  | 56.565  | 565.650  | 5656.498  |

Two things to read off this table, both load-bearing later:

- **The headline regime, 1 fb⁻¹, is an experiment expecting ≈ 5.7 events in total.** This is the number meant whenever this note says "~6 events". It is the *total* S+B+I count — the signal's net contribution is actually **negative** (0.310 − 0.606 = −0.296), because destructive interference beats the tiny signal-squared term.
- **The interference is large compared with the signal** ($|\sigma_I| \approx 2\sigma_S$). That single fact will break a regularity condition in §7.

### 2.4 What is in the samples

The events are **parton-level MC**: a Monte Carlo generator produced fixed-order gg → 4ℓ events — no parton shower, no hadronisation, no detector simulation. Each stored event carries six four-vectors (two purely longitudinal incoming partons and the four final-state leptons) and, crucially, the **squared matrix elements** for each component (`msq_sig`, `msq_int`, `msq_bkg`, `msq_sbi`). Those matrix elements are what make a single sample serve *every* value of μ, by per-event reweighting:

$$w(\mu)=w_{\rm SM}\cdot\frac{\mu\,{\rm msq}_{\rm sig}+\sqrt{\mu}\,{\rm msq}_{\rm int}+{\rm msq}_{\rm bkg}}{{\rm msq}_{\rm sbi}}$$

Reweighting works because every component hypothesis populates the *same* phase space: changing μ changes how much each phase-space point weighs, never which points exist. The samples also carry 45 BSM matrix-element variations on a (c6, ct, cg) = 5×3×3 EFT grid — the same trick, pointed at a later phase of the project. One verifiable kinematic consequence of the fixed-order gg initial state, worth knowing because it matters for the dimension counting below: the four-lepton system has exactly zero transverse momentum (no initial-state radiation to recoil against).

### 2.5 What the analyses actually see

| Analysis | Observable(s) | Dimension |
|---|---|---|
| Histogram | m₄ℓ (the four-lepton invariant mass) | 1 |
| NSBI | the four leptons' kinematics: (pt, η, φ, E) for each of ℓ₁…ℓ₄ | 16 network inputs |

The **16** quoted throughout this project is therefore the **input width of the neural networks** — four leptons × four coordinates — and nothing more mysterious than that. Be precise about it, though, because §§7–8 turn on dimensionality: **16 is not the dimension of the physical phase space.** The coordinates are redundant: for massless leptons E is fixed by (pt, η), removing 4; the vanishing four-lepton transverse momentum (§2.4) removes 2; overall azimuthal invariance removes 1. What remains is **≈ 9 genuine degrees of freedom** — exactly the size of the standard four-lepton basis (five decay angles plus m(Z₁), m(Z₂), m₄ℓ, y₄ℓ). So the networks are fed 16 redundant coordinates spanning about 9 real dimensions. Nine is still hopelessly many for 5.7 events, so every argument below survives — but the honest number for the *phase space* is 9, not 16.

## 3. Weighted events, and how toy pseudo-experiments are made

This section exists because "we threw toys" hides a beautiful and slightly subtle construction, and because the whole project's comparator — the median of toys — inherits its meaning from it.

### 3.1 What a weighted MC sample is

A weighted sample is a list of N_MC simulated events, each carrying kinematics $x_i$ and a weight $w_i \ge 0$, normalised so that the weights *sum to the physics expectation*:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm MC}} w_i \cdot L=\nu_{\rm tot} \quad\text{(the expected event count at luminosity } L\text{)}$$

Read $w_i L$ as: "in an actual experiment of luminosity L, this simulated configuration would occur, on average, $w_i L$ times". Typically $w_i L \ll 1$ — the simulation covers phase space much more densely than a real dataset would populate it. That is the point of weighted MC: dense coverage of *shape*, with the *rate* carried by the normalisation.

### 3.2 The recipe

A toy pseudo-experiment is generated by **one independent Poisson draw per event**:

$$w_i^{\rm toy}\sim\mathcal{P}(\lambda=w_i\,L)\qquad\text{independently for each } i$$

The toy *is* the collection of events with $w_i^{\rm toy} > 0$, each counted with its drawn multiplicity. Most events draw 0 and vanish; some draw 1; occasionally one draws 2. Figure 1, left: the grey stems are the weights, the blue dots are one toy. Right: the same construction seen through a histogram — the Asimov template (weights summed per bin) with three toys around it.

![Toy generation: per-event Poisson draws (left) and the binned view (right)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/toy_mechanics.png)

### 3.3 Why this is *exactly* right, in three steps

The recipe looks ad hoc; it is not. It is an exact sample from the extended likelihood, and the proof is three properties of the Poisson distribution.

**Step 1 — the model is a superposition of independent point processes.** "Events of type $x_i$ occur at rate $w_i L$" defines, for each i, an independent Poisson process; the physical dataset is their superposition. Drawing $w_i^{\rm toy} \sim \mathcal{P}(w_i L)$ independently per event is a literal simulation of that statement.

**Step 2 — the total rate comes out right.** A sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson with the summed mean:

$$N_{\rm toy}=\sum_i w_i^{\rm toy}\sim\mathcal{P}\Big(\sum_i w_i L\Big)=\mathcal{P}(\nu_{\rm tot})$$

So the toy's total event count fluctuates exactly as the extended likelihood's rate term says it must.

**Step 3 — the shape comes out right.** Conditional on the total count N_toy, the drawn events are distributed over the sample in proportion to their weights (the classic "thinning" property of Poisson processes) — i.e. as N_toy i.i.d. draws from the normalised density that the weighted sample represents. That is exactly the shape term of the extended likelihood.

Together: one Poisson draw per weight = one honest realisation of $\mathcal{P}(n|\nu)\prod_i p(x_i)$. Rate and shape fluctuate **coherently and simultaneously** — a toy with an upward count fluctuation has it in *specific places*, not spread uniformly. No separate machinery for the two terms, no double counting.

### 3.4 What toys are *not*

- **Not a bootstrap.** The bootstrap resamples the observed dataset with replacement, holding N fixed at the observed count; it estimates the sampling variability of a statistic *given the data you got*. Toys sample fresh datasets *from the model*, count and all. For coverage and Asimov-vs-median questions the model's own fluctuations are the object of study, so toys are the right tool and the bootstrap is not.
- **Not an estimate of model or training uncertainty.** Toys fluctuate the *data*; they hold the analysis — including any trained network — perfectly fixed. The team's phrasing of this (April meeting) is worth engraving: *toys estimate the statistical uncertainty of the data; ensembling estimates the bias and variance of the network — a different uncertainty entirely.* §13 returns to what ensembles do.
- **Not luminosity-agnostic.** The draw is from $\mathcal{P}(w_i L)$: the same weighted sample yields honest 1 fb⁻¹ toys (sparse, mostly-empty) and honest 1000 fb⁻¹ toys (dense) just by changing L. This is how one sweep of L is possible without regenerating anything.

### 3.5 The protocol, and the definition that must be owned

For each toy, the full analysis (histogram or NSBI) produces a −2Δln L scan over the μ grid; the comparator is the **pointwise-per-μ median** of the toy curves, with a quantile band, overlaid on the Asimov curve.

> ⚠️ "The median of the toys" is a definition, not a given. At least three inequivalent objects deserve the name: (1) the pointwise median of the toy curves referenced to a common baseline; (2) the pointwise median after each toy is min-subtracted (referenced to its own $\hat{\mu}$ — the convention that matches the scalar-statistic theorem of §5, since $t_\mu$ is defined relative to $\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mu})$); (3) the median of a *scalar summary* of each toy (its interval width, say) — which is what the Asimov theorem actually addresses. These coincide in the asymptotic limit and need not coincide at six events: a pointwise median of non-parabolic curves is not the curve of any single experiment, and can even fail to be parabolic itself. Tei flagged the same subtlety from the normalisation side — individual toy curves can run below the nominal Asimov curve, so the vertical axis is not automatically comparable between toys until a reference is fixed. **Every quantitative claim must name the convention it used.** Which convention is the *interesting* one is a genuinely open question (§18).

## 4. Likelihood-ratio inference: Wilks and Wald

The statistical machinery is frequentist and likelihood-based throughout. For a dataset of n observed events with observables x₁…xₙ, the **extended likelihood** is

$$\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\underbrace{\mathcal{P}\big(n\mid\nu(\mu)\big)}_{\text{rate term}}\;\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(x_i|\mu)}_{\text{shape term}}$$

and inference uses the likelihood-ratio statistic

$$t_\mu=-2\ln\lambda(\mu),\qquad\lambda(\mu)=\frac{\mathcal{L}(\mu)}{\mathcal{L}(\hat{\mu})}$$

with $\hat{\mu}$ the maximum-likelihood estimate. (No nuisance parameters in this project yet; with them, λ becomes the profile likelihood ratio and the story is unchanged.)

**Where the two theorems come from — a sketch worth internalising.** Expand $\ln\mathcal{L}$ around its maximum. The first derivative vanishes there by definition, so to second order

$$-2\ln\lambda(\mu)\approx(\mu-\hat{\mu})^2\cdot\Big[-\partial_\mu^2\ln\mathcal{L}\Big]_{\hat{\mu}}=\frac{(\mu-\hat{\mu})^2}{\sigma^2}$$

which is **Wald's approximation**: the scan curve is a parabola whose curvature *is* the inverse variance of the estimate. If, additionally, $\hat{\mu}$ is Gaussian around the truth with that same σ — which a central limit theorem must supply — then $(\mu-\hat{\mu})/\sigma$ is standard normal under the hypothesis μ, and its square, $t_\mu$, follows a **χ² distribution with one degree of freedom**. That is **Wilks' theorem**. Every familiar rule follows: Δ(−2ln L) = 1 ⇔ 68% interval, Δ = 3.84 ⇔ 95%, significance = √t₀.

So the entire edifice rests on two ingredients: *the log-likelihood is locally quadratic* and *the estimator is Gaussian*. Both are large-sample statements. Figure 2 shows what is bought and what its loss looks like — the right panel is an exact, plain six-event counting experiment, no interference and nothing exotic, and the parabola is already a poor description across the scan range.

![Asymptotic parabola vs an exact six-event scan](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/wilks_wald.png)

> ⚠️ Nothing in either theorem survives by fiat at 5.7 expected events. Whether the conclusions survive *approximately* is a quantitative question — and it is the question of this project. §7 unpacks exactly which conditions must hold, because "large sample" is the phrase that has to be dismantled before it can be transported from a histogram to a neural network.

## 5. The Asimov dataset and what is actually proven

The **Asimov dataset** (Cowan, Cranmer, Gross, Vitells 2011, Sec. 3.2) is the artificial dataset in which every observable takes exactly its expected value: every histogram bin holds its expected yield, the total count equals ν(μ′) for the assumed truth μ′. Its defining property: fitting it returns the truth exactly,

$$\hat{\mu}_A=\mu'$$

**The median property, derived in three lines.** Work inside the Wald approximation, testing hypothesis μ when the truth is μ′:

1. $t_\mu \approx (\mu-\hat{\mu})^2/\sigma^2$, and under the truth, $\hat{\mu} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu', \sigma)$.
2. $t_\mu$ is a *monotone function* of $\hat{\mu}$ on either side of μ; a monotone function maps the median of the input to the median of the output. The median of $\hat{\mu}$ is μ′.
3. Therefore the median of $t_\mu$ is its value at $\hat{\mu}=\mu'$ — which, by the defining property above, is exactly the Asimov value $t_{\mu,A}=(\mu-\mu')^2/\sigma^2$.

Median expected significance without a single toy: that theorem is why "fit the Asimov, skip the toys" became standard practice in HEP.

> 🎯 **What is proven vs. what we test.** The proven statement is a median property of the *scalar test statistic*, derived *inside* the Wald approximation — step 1 assumed the parabola and the Gaussian estimator before the argument even began. The property this project tests — pointwise equality of the whole Asimov *curve* with the median of toy *curves*, at ~6 expected events — is strictly stronger, and it is **our own operational definition** (attribute it to this study, not to Cowan et al.). Its violation therefore contradicts no theorem. It demonstrates something else: a regime assumed asymptotic is not.

## 6. Coverage: what an error bar actually promises

Everything in this project — the Asimov check, the overconfidence language, the SBI literature of §13, the mitigation designs of §17 — ultimately talks about one property. It deserves its own section.

### 6.1 The definition

A procedure that turns data into an interval has **coverage** 1−α if, under repeated experiments with true value μ′, the reported interval contains μ′ a fraction 1−α of the time:

$$P\big(\mu'\in[\hat{\mu}_{\rm lo},\hat{\mu}_{\rm hi}]\big)=1-\alpha\quad\text{for every }\mu'$$

Three things to notice. Coverage is a property of the **procedure**, not of any single interval — a given interval either contains the truth or it doesn't, and you will never know which. The probability is over *repetitions of the experiment* — which is why toys (§3) are the natural instrument for measuring it. And the promise is **for every μ′** — a procedure can cover at one truth and fail at another, so coverage must be *mapped*, not spot-checked.

**Vocabulary.** Exact coverage = calibrated. Coverage above nominal = **conservative** (valid but wasteful: intervals longer than necessary). Coverage below nominal = **overconfident** — the failure mode this project is about: intervals shorter than their stated confidence warrants. Figure 3 makes it concrete: same central values, intervals reported 45% too short, and the "68%" procedure captures the truth less than half the time.

![Calibrated vs overconfident interval procedures](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/coverage.png)

### 6.2 The Neyman construction — coverage with no asymptotics at all

Neyman (1937) showed how to build intervals whose coverage is guaranteed **by construction**, with no appeal to large samples. The recipe, step by step:

1. Choose a test statistic T(data; μ).
2. For **each** candidate value of μ, find the acceptance region — the set of T values containing probability 1−α *under that* μ (in practice: throw toys at μ, take the empirical quantiles of T).
3. Observe the data; compute T(data; μ) for every μ.
4. Report the confidence set: every μ whose acceptance region contains the observed T.

Coverage is then automatic: whatever the truth is, step 2 made its acceptance region contain the data with probability 1−α, so the truth enters the reported set with probability 1−α. No parabola, no Gaussianity, no large-N — the guarantee is exact, at any sample size, for any statistic. What the choice of statistic and ordering controls is not validity but **efficiency**: bad choices give valid-but-long intervals (the Feldman–Cousins ordering is the standard refinement).

**The connection that organises this whole note:** the familiar rule "cut the scan at Δ(−2ln L) = 1" is nothing but the Neyman construction with two shortcuts — statistic fixed to $t_\mu$ and the per-μ toy quantiles replaced by the χ² quantile that Wilks promises. When the asymptotic conditions hold, the shortcut is free. When they fail, the χ² quantile is simply the *wrong threshold* — and the full, toy-based construction is the fallback that always works, at the price of throwing toys per hypothesis. Keep this in mind at §17: the most robust mitigation on the table is essentially "stop taking the shortcut".

### 6.3 Coverage vs the Asimov check

The Asimov-vs-median comparison never looks at the truth: it compares two of the procedure's *own* sensitivity predictions against each other (expectation-level fit vs toy ensemble). A coverage measurement confronts intervals *with* the truth. They are different diagnostics probing the same underlying disease — a statistic whose sampling distribution is not what the analysis assumes — and they can disagree: a procedure can pass the Asimov check and still miscover (nothing in "Asimov = median" pins the *absolute* scale of either). That independence is a feature: run both.

## 7. When does the Asimov property hold? The four conditions

This is the section the rest of the project hangs on, so it is worth being pedantic.

The folklore answer — *"you need enough events in every bin"* — is not the condition. It is a **binned proxy** for one of the conditions, in one particular representation, and that is exactly why it fails to transport to NSBI: an NSBI likelihood has no bins, so the folklore rule has nothing to say about it — and one is tempted to conclude, wrongly, that a large *total* event count is enough. Unpacking "asymptotic" properly gives four separate requirements: three behind Wilks/Wald, and a fourth we impose on ourselves by testing curves rather than scalars.

### (A) Regularity of the model at the truth

The classical conditions require the Fisher information to be finite, positive and **non-vanishing** at the true parameter; the truth to lie in the **interior** of the parameter space; and the parameter to be **identifiable** — the map from μ to the distribution locally one-to-one. If the information vanishes at the truth, the likelihood is locally flatter than quadratic, $\hat{\mu}$ is not asymptotically Gaussian, and its variance does not scale like 1/N. No amount of data repairs this: it is a property of the *model*, not the sample.

**This condition is not automatic in our model, and the culprit is the interference.** Differentiate the total rate of §2.2:

$$\frac{d\nu_{\rm tot}}{d\mu}=L\left(\sigma_S+\frac{\sigma_I}{2\sqrt{\mu}}\right)$$

Because σ_I is *negative*, this derivative has a zero: the rate is stationary — minimal — at

$$\mu^*=\left(\frac{\sigma_I}{2\,\sigma_S}\right)^{2}\approx 0.957$$

**essentially on top of the Standard Model point we are measuring** (plug in the §2.3 cross-sections; the derivative at μ = 1 is $\sigma_S+\sigma_I/2 \approx 0.0067$ fb, a factor ~46 below σ_S itself). Two consequences follow by algebra alone: the event *count* carries almost no first-order information about μ near the SM, and the rate is locally **two-to-one** around μ\* — two different μ on either side of the fold predict the same total rate, a local identifiability failure. Figure 4 shows both.

![The interference rate fold](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/rate_fold.png)

This is a statement about the *parametrisation and the physics* — not about binning, machine learning, or sample size. It applies equally to histogram and NSBI, and, being an information deficit that data cannot fill, it becomes *more* visible as luminosity grows, not less.

### (B) A central limit theorem for the score

This is the condition the folklore is groping at. The relevant object is the **score** — the derivative of the log-likelihood, a *sum over events*:

$$U(\mu)=\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mu}=-\nu'(\mu)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln\big[\nu(\mu)\,p(x_i|\mu)\big]$$

Asymptotic normality of $\hat{\mu}$ comes from a CLT applied to this sum. For the CLT to bite, two things must hold: **many contributing terms**, and **no small subset dominating** (formally a Lindeberg-type condition: finite per-event variance, no heavy tail in the contributions).

Watch what this means in each representation:

- **In a histogram**, every event in bin b contributes the *same* score term $\partial_\mu\ln\nu_b(\mu)$. The sum collapses onto a handful of distinct values weighted by bin counts, and the CLT's demands reduce to "each bin's count is large enough to be Gaussian". *That* is where "≥ 10 per bin" comes from: a sufficient condition, in one representation, for condition (B). Not a definition of asymptotics.
- **In NSBI there are no bins.** Every event contributes its own $\partial_\mu\ln r(x_i|\mu)$ — a continuous quantity that can be arbitrarily large for an event in a sparse corner of the ≈9-dimensional space. The sum can be dominated by a handful of events even when n looks comfortable.

The representation-independent invariant is therefore **the effective number of events in the score sum**, e.g. the Kish effective sample size

$$N_{\rm eff}=\frac{\big(\sum_i u_i\big)^2}{\sum_i u_i^2},\qquad u_i=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln r(x_i|\mu)$$

which equals n for equal contributions and collapses toward 1 when one event dominates. **A 100-event NSBI dataset whose log-ratio sum is carried by a few events is, statistically, a few-event experiment.** This is the honest translation of "enough events per bin" into a language a per-event likelihood can hear — and it is what a dimensionality/occupancy study is really probing. Figure 5 shows two datasets with identical N and wildly different N_eff.

![Effective sample size of the score](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/neff_score.png)

### (C) Correct specification

Wilks and Wald assume the likelihood being maximised **is the true likelihood of the data**. This is so routinely free that it is easy to forget it is an assumption at all.

- For a **histogram** it is free: bin counts are unbiased estimators of bin expectations by construction, so the binned Poisson likelihood is the exact likelihood of the binned data. (Binning throws information away; what remains is perfectly modelled.)
- For **NSBI it is never exactly true.** The per-event ratio is *learned* from finite training data; the scanned likelihood is a *different function* from the true one. Under mis-specification, the MLE converges to a "pseudo-true" value and the curvature of the wrong likelihood is not the variance of anything.

The decisive detail: **the estimation error is frozen into the analysis.** It is the same $\hat{r}$ for every event, every toy, every luminosity — it does not average away as data accumulate, the way statistical noise does. This is the one condition *structurally unavailable* to NSBI, and the reason no mitigation can consist of "collect more events".

### (D) Curve-level quadraticity — our own extra requirement

Conditions (A)–(C) suffice for the scalar theorem of §5. The property we test is stronger: pointwise agreement of whole scan *curves* across the window. That additionally needs the parabola to hold *over the range scanned* (not just near the minimum) and is sensitive to the physical boundary μ ≥ 0 (at low luminosity the minimum can sit near or beyond it, where the Wald form is invalid). Keep (D) separate as a matter of intellectual hygiene: when the curve-level test fails, no theorem of Wilks or Cowan has been violated.

### Summary table

| | Condition | Fails when… | Histogram | NSBI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Regularity at the truth | the rate is stationary in μ — as here, at μ\* ≈ 0.957, by destructive interference | at risk | at risk (same parametrisation) |
| B | CLT for the score | few *effective* events carry the sum — sparse cells, heavy-tailed log-ratios | buyable: widen bins | not buyable: no bin-width knob |
| C | Correct specification | the likelihood is *estimated* rather than known | free by construction | structurally violated |
| D | Quadraticity across the scan | non-parabolic curves; $\hat{\mu}$ near the μ = 0 boundary | at risk at low L | at risk at low L |

The three mechanisms this project can plausibly find are (A), (B), (C) — and they are *distinguishable*, because they respond differently to luminosity: (A) grows with L, (B) shrinks with L, (C) is flat in L. That single row of behaviour is the experimental design hiding inside the theory.

## 8. Why a histogram can buy the conditions and NSBI cannot

With §7 in hand, the project's framing hypothesis is a two-line argument.

A **histogram analysis** compresses each event to one observable and bins it. The analyst controls the bin width: if a bin fails condition (B), merge bins until it doesn't. Coarse binning discards information but *buys asymptotic validity* — and costs nothing in condition (C), because binned counts remain exactly modelled however coarse the bins. That knob is why the histogram behaves at every luminosity.

An **NSBI analysis** evaluates a per-event ratio at each event's exact point in the ≈9-dimensional space — the limit of an infinitely finely binned histogram. The effective "cell" around an event contains, in expectation, far less than one event; and there is **no bin-widening knob**, because giving up resolution is precisely what NSBI exists to avoid. Worse: the act of replacing bins by a learned function is itself what breaks condition (C).

> 💡 **Analogy.** A histogram lets you choose the bucket size; NSBI hands every event its own bucket. You can always make buckets big enough that each holds a crowd — but a bucket sized for one occupant is never crowded, no matter how many buckets there are.

The consequence, and the hypothesis worth falsifying: *for high-dimensional per-event analyses, the non-asymptotic regime extends to far larger total event counts than one-dimensional intuition suggests.* The cleanest test takes machine learning **out of the picture entirely**: add axes to a plain histogram. If a multi-dimensional histogram starts failing the Asimov property at the cell occupancies where NSBI fails, the mechanism is condition (B) and the network is exonerated; if it does not, condition (C) is implicated. Either way the experiment is decisive — which is why it leads the research programme (§17.4).

## 9. Learning the likelihood ratio: CARL and NSBI, from first principles

The densities $p_S, p_B, p_{\rm SBI}$ in the full kinematic space are not known in closed form — only samples exist. Simulation-based inference solves this with a classifier, and the fact that this works is a theorem worth deriving once in your life.

### 9.1 The likelihood-ratio trick, derived

Train a classifier s(x) ∈ (0,1) to separate samples of $p_{\rm num}$ (label y = 1) from samples of $p_{\rm ref}$ (label y = 0), mixed in equal proportions, by minimising the standard binary cross-entropy

$$\mathbb{E}\big[-y\ln s(x)-(1-y)\ln(1-s(x))\big]$$

Because the loss is an expectation over x, it can be minimised **pointwise**: at each x, the integrand is $-\pi_1(x)\ln s-(1-\pi_1(x))\ln(1-s)$ with $\pi_1(x)=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm num}(x)+p_{\rm ref}(x)}$ the local probability that a sample at x carries label 1. Differentiate with respect to s and set to zero:

$$-\frac{\pi_1}{s}+\frac{1-\pi_1}{1-s}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^*(x)=\pi_1(x)=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm num}(x)+p_{\rm ref}(x)}$$

Rearranged, the *optimally trained* classifier encodes the density ratio exactly:

$$\frac{s^*(x)}{1-s^*(x)}=\frac{p_{\rm num}(x)}{p_{\rm ref}(x)}$$

A *discriminative* network — the thing deep learning is best at — has produced a *density ratio*, with no density estimation anywhere. This is CARL (Cranmer–Pavez–Louppe). Note what the derivation quietly assumed: infinite data, unlimited capacity, exact optimisation. A real network satisfies none of the three, which is why CARL's second ingredient — **calibration**, diagnosing and correcting the residual distortion of $\hat{s}/(1-\hat{s})$ — is part of the method's name and not an optional extra. It is also exactly where condition (C) of §7 enters: the gap between $s^*$ and $\hat{s}$ *is* the mis-specification.

### 9.2 The NSBI construction used here

Choose the reference $p_{\rm ref}=p_B$ and train **two** CARL networks: one for $p_S/p_B$, one for $p_{\rm SBI}/p_B$. Feeding both into the mixture formula of §2.2 gives the full μ-dependent ratio for *any* μ:

$$\frac{p_{\rm SBI}(x|\mu)}{p_B(x)}=\frac{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S\,\frac{p_S}{p_B}(x)+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}\,\frac{p_{\rm SBI}}{p_B}(x)+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}{(\mu-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\nu_{\rm SBI}+(1-\sqrt{\mu})\,\nu_B}$$

The arbitrary reference cancels in ratios of ratios, so its choice is harmless; and the μ-dependence lives entirely in *known coefficients*, so the networks are trained **once** — on balanced, shape-only samples carrying **no luminosity information** — and every hypothesis is evaluated by re-mixing the same two outputs. Luminosity enters only at inference. Hold that thought: it is the seed of hypothesis 3 in §12.

## 10. The two likelihoods, side by side

Both analyses are extended-likelihood fits of the same μ.

**Histogram** — ten log-spaced m₄ℓ bins:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\prod_{\rm bins}\mathcal{P}\big(n_b\mid\nu_b(\mu)\big)$$

The product of per-bin Poissons automatically contains the total-rate information (the bin expectations sum to the total). Nothing needs adding.

**NSBI** — a per-event shape product times one global rate term:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mu)=\mathcal{P}\big(n\mid\nu(\mu)\big)\prod_{\rm events}\frac{p_{\rm SBI}(x_i|\mu)}{p_{\rm ref}(x_i)},\qquad\nu(\mu)=L\big(\mu\,\sigma_S+\sqrt{\mu}\,\sigma_I+\sigma_B\big)$$

Two structural remarks, both essential. Dividing the shape product by $p_{\rm ref}$ is harmless — μ-independent, cancels in −2Δln L. And **the rate term is analytic**: computed from known cross-sections, exact, never touched by a network. The networks supply shape information and nothing else.

> 🎯 **The rate term is where the luminosity dependence lives.** The shape term gains information per event; the rate term gains information as ν(μ) grows with L — and it is exact. As luminosity increases, an exactly-known, well-behaved term takes over from an approximate one: the natural explanation for why an NSBI analysis would agree with its Asimov at very high luminosity and disagree below. Corollary: an NSBI likelihood *without* its rate term is pure shape — pure neural network — and inherits every pathology of the network with nothing to dilute it.
>
> And note the tension with §7(A): near the SM point the rate term of *this particular model* is nearly flat in μ, so "the rate term takes over" cannot be the whole story close to μ = 1 — it constrains the far tails, where ν(μ) finally varies, more than the neighbourhood of the truth.

## 11. The founding observation

Schematically (Figure 7 — a redrawn cartoon, not measured curves): in the **histogram** analysis the median of the toy scans tracks the Asimov scan at every luminosity, band and all. In the **NSBI** analysis at low luminosity the toy median is systematically **steeper** than the Asimov — each pseudo-experiment claims *more* sensitivity than the expectation-level fit predicts — with the discrepancy shrinking as luminosity grows and vanishing when the rate term dominates.

![The founding observation, schematic](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/asimov_vs_toys_schematic.png)

Read the direction carefully, because it decides the vocabulary: the *toys* are the steeper curves, so each toy analysis is **overconfident** relative to the Asimov expectation — the Asimov *understates* what the toys claim. In coverage language (§6): if the toy-level curvature is too large, the Δ = 1 intervals cut from toy curves are too short, and the procedure under-covers. That controlled contrast is what makes the observation worth explaining rather than dismissing.

## 12. Why could NSBI be overconfident? Hypotheses

Four candidate mechanisms, not mutually exclusive — each mapped to a condition from §7, which is what turns them from vibes into separable experiments.

1. **NN mis-specification** *(condition C)*. The learned ratio deviates from the true one; a wrong likelihood is being scanned, and no theorem governs the curvature of a wrong likelihood. The observation that agreement improves exactly when the exact rate term dominates points at the NN-supplied piece.
2. **No unbiased NN estimator exists** *(condition C, sharpened)*. Bin counts are unbiased estimators of bin expectations by construction; a trained network is an unbiased estimator of nothing, ensembled or not. Some bias in the learned log-ratio is unavoidable and its effect on curvature is uncontrolled.
3. **Training is blind to sample-size fluctuations** *(condition B, seen from the estimator's side)*. The networks are trained once, on a single large balanced sample, with no luminosity information (§9.2). The learned ratio is a statement about *densities*; nothing in the training objective represents what a six-event dataset does to a shape estimate. In the team's phrasing: the network *"doesn't realise how limited it is by the statistics of the dataset"* — it has literally never been shown a small dataset.
4. **SBI is generically overconfident** *(the external prior)*. The ML literature has documented, across SBI algorithm families including CARL's, that approximate posteriors are systematically less conservative than the truth unless explicitly diagnosed and corrected (§13).

> 💡 The tension worth holding on to: hypotheses 1–2 say the network is **wrong**; hypothesis 3 bites even for a *perfectly* learned ratio, because it concerns what a finite dataset does to the statistic's sampling distribution, not the quality of the density estimate. Disentangling them is precisely what the research programme is for — and §7's luminosity-dependence row is the instrument.

## 13. What the SBI literature already knows: overconfidence, ensembles, and balanced training

### 13.1 The trust-crisis result

The project's frequentist observation has a Bayesian twin, documented at benchmark scale. Hermans et al. (Ref. 5) present extensive empirical evidence that simulation-based inference algorithms "can produce computationally unfaithful posterior approximations", and the finding is family-wide — verbatim: "all benchmarked algorithms – (Sequential) Neural Posterior Estimation, (Sequential) Neural Ratio Estimation, Sequential Neural Likelihood and variants of Approximate Bayesian Computation – can yield overconfident posterior approximations, which makes them unreliable for scientific use cases". Neural ratio estimation — CARL's family — is on that list explicitly. This is not an anecdote about one badly tuned network; it is systematic behaviour of the method class across simulators and budgets, worst at small ones.

**Their diagnostic is coverage, in exactly §6's sense.** For a posterior approximation $\hat{p}(\vartheta|x)$ and credible level 1−α, let $\Theta(1-\alpha)$ be the highest-posterior-density region integrating to 1−α; the **expected coverage probability**

$$\mathrm{ECP}(1-\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta,x)}\Big[\mathbb{1}\big(\vartheta\in\Theta_{\hat{p}(\vartheta\mid x)}(1-\alpha)\big)\Big]$$

is measured by simulating pairs (ϑ\*, x) from the joint and asking how often the truth lands in the claimed region. Faithful: ECP = 1−α at every level. Conservative: ≥. **Overconfident: <** — and the BNRE paper itself notes that this quantity "can be interpreted as the expected frequentist coverage": prior-averaged over ϑ\*, it *is* §6's coverage. The Bayesian and frequentist literatures are diagnosing the same disease with the same instrument.

### 13.2 Ensembles — what they fix and what they cannot

The mitigation Hermans et al. themselves report is **ensembling**: train M networks that differ only in initialisation (and possibly data shuffling), and combine their outputs. The canonical reference is Deep Ensembles (Ref. 16). What is it actually estimating? Training a network is itself a noisy algorithm — random initialisation, stochastic optimisation, one finite training sample — so the learned $\hat{r}$ is one draw from a distribution over functions. The ensemble spread estimates that **model/training uncertainty**, and averaging over members smooths out the individual networks' idiosyncratic errors: a variance reduction on the estimator, hence more faithful (less overconfident) inference on average.

Now place ensembles against §7 and against the toys of §3, because the three-way distinction is the most commonly confused point in this whole subject:

- **Toys** fluctuate the *data*; they estimate statistical uncertainty at fixed analysis. (Condition B territory.)
- **Ensembles** fluctuate the *training*; they estimate the estimator's variance at fixed data distribution. (The variance half of condition C.)
- **Neither** addresses the estimator's *bias* — the part of condition C that averaging over ensemble members cannot remove, because every member is trained toward the same wrong-at-finite-capacity optimum. And neither teaches the analysis that a six-event dataset deserves humility — hypothesis 3 stands untouched by both.

The team's own version of this (April meeting) is worth preserving verbatim in spirit: *ensembling would be the same move as the toys but for a different uncertainty — toys cover the statistics of the data, ensembling catches the bias/variance of the network* — together with the sharper observation that an **un**-optimised single network makes the low-luminosity overconfidence *easier* to see, because tuning the network attacks neither the sample-size blindness nor the frozen bias.

### 13.3 Balanced NRE — buying conservatism in the objective

The sharper structural fix in the literature is **Balanced Neural Ratio Estimation** (BNRE, Ref. 13). In NRE, a classifier $\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)$ separates joint samples $p(\vartheta,x)$ from product-of-marginals samples $p(\vartheta)p(x)$; the Bayes-optimal classifier encodes the likelihood-to-evidence ratio (same derivation as §9.1, different pair of densities). BNRE restricts the hypothesis space to **balanced** classifiers — those satisfying

$$\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta,x)}\big[\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{p(\vartheta)p(x)}\big[\hat{d}(\vartheta,x)\big]=1$$

Three theorems make the restriction attractive: every balanced classifier is, *in expectation*, less confident than the Bayes-optimal one; and the Bayes-optimal classifier is itself balanced — so the restriction costs nothing at convergence: same optimum, conservative tendency at finite budget. Implemented as one penalty term added to the classification loss; no extra networks, no extra compute.

> 💡 **Why this matters operationally here.** Our two CARL networks are binary classifiers of exactly this type. A BNRE-style balancing penalty is a drop-in modification of the CARL training loss — one line — which turns "conservative training objectives" from a literature pointer into a cheap experiment, and gives every other mitigation a conservative baseline to beat (§17.5).

## 14. The Bartlett correction, from first principles

### 14.1 The idea

In finite samples $t_\mu$ is not χ²-distributed. The lowest-order symptom sits in its **mean**: a χ² with one degree of freedom has mean exactly 1, but a systematic expansion of the LR statistic in powers of 1/n (n = sample size) gives

$$\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]=1+\frac{b}{n}+\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})$$

with b a model-dependent constant. Bartlett's move (1937) is disarmingly simple — rescale the statistic by its actual mean:

$$t'_\mu=\frac{t_\mu}{\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]}$$

so that $\mathbb{E}[t'_\mu]=1$ **by construction**. The non-trivial content — due to Lawley — is that fixing the first moment repairs the *entire distribution* at the next order: for regular parametric families, the χ² approximation error of the corrected statistic improves from $\mathcal{O}(1/n)$ to $\mathcal{O}(1/n^2)$. Not just the mean — every quantile moves toward χ². That across-the-board improvement is what earns the name "correction" rather than "normalisation", and it is remarkable: one scalar, measured or computed, buys an order in 1/n.

### 14.2 Getting the one scalar

Two routes to $\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]$. **Analytic**, where the model permits an expansion — Cowan (2019, Ref. 9) works a HEP example (his gamma-variance/error-on-error model, $\mathbb{E}[t_\mu]=1+3r^2+cr^4$) and notes the method "has received some limited notice in Particle Physics". **Monte Carlo**, in his words: "one could in principle find the expectation value E[t_μ] by the Monte Carlo method". For an NN-defined statistic no analytic expansion exists, so the MC route is the only one — and it is nearly free here, because a toy ensemble already produces, at every (μ, L), the full empirical distribution of $t_\mu$; its **mean** is a by-product of scans run anyway. (Note the division of labour: Bartlett needs the *mean*; the Asimov comparator uses the *median*. Same ensemble, two different summaries.)

### 14.3 The fine print — which is also the payoff

Bartlett's guarantee presumes conditions (A) and (C) of §7: a *regular*, *correctly specified* model. Where the information is nearly stationary (our fold) the regular-family theorems simply do not apply — an empirical mean-rescaling may still help at a given quantile, but it arrives without its warranty. And no rescaling of a statistic repairs the statistic's *model* being wrong.

> 🎯 That limitation is exactly what makes the correction **diagnostic**, and this is the most useful sentence in the section: *if a Bartlett-type rescaling reconciles Asimov and toys, the failure was distributional — condition (B) territory; if it does not, the estimator itself is implicated — condition (C).* One extra curve per panel, no new training, and it separates the two leading hypotheses of §12.

## 15. MC statistical uncertainties: the Barlow–Beeston idea

There is a second finite sample hiding in every template analysis: the **simulation**. The bin expectations $\nu_b(\mu)$ (or the learned ratio) are *estimated* from finite MC, so they carry statistical errors of their own — and a likelihood that treats them as exact is, once again, quietly overconfident.

**The full treatment** (Barlow & Beeston 1993, Ref. 14): promote each bin's true MC expectation to a nuisance parameter, constrained by the observed MC count as an auxiliary Poisson measurement, and profile. The likelihood then *knows* the templates are uncertain: bins with poor MC support contribute less information, intervals widen accordingly, and the widening is largest exactly where occupancy is worst — tying this directly to condition (B).

**The "lite" simplification**, standard in modern HEP fits: one multiplicative nuisance γ_b per bin (rather than per bin per source), constrained by the bin's **effective MC sample size**

$$n_{\rm eff}=\frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}$$

— the same Kish formula as §7(B), now applied to weights rather than score terms; for weighted MC it is the honest answer to "how many events is this bin really based on". The per-bin profile has a closed form, so the machinery adds no minimiser and negligible cost.

**Why it belongs in this project's toolbox:** it is the conservative correction that attacks the *simulation-side* part of condition (C) — the one piece of "the likelihood is not exact" that has a classical, guaranteed-valid fix. It will not repair a learned ratio's bias, but it removes one alibi: any overconfidence that survives MC-stat profiling cannot be blamed on finite simulation.

### 15.1 The network-side counterpart: wifi ensembles (the April meeting's method, identified 2026-07-13)

Barlow–Beeston answers "the *templates* were estimated from finite MC". Its network-side counterpart answers "the *learned ratio* was estimated from finite data" — and the April meeting's *"new Wi-Fi ensemble method"*, with which MC-stat uncertainties are *"trivial to apply"*, turns out to be exactly that. The transcription was never garbled: the method is **wᵢfᵢ ensembles** (Ref. 17, Benevedes & Thaler 2025), a pun on weights **wᵢ** times basis functions **fᵢ**.

**The construction.** Train M networks $f_i(x)$ (bootstrap resamples, or a partition of the training set), then **freeze them** and treat them as a *basis* rather than as M competing answers. Model the log density ratio as a linear combination, $\log \tilde r(x|w) = \sum_i w_i f_i(x)$, and fit only the M weights on a **held-out** sample with a convex (symmetrised MLC) loss. Because the loss is linear in $w$, everything downstream is closed-form: a sandwich covariance $C = V^{-1} U V^{-1}$ ($V$ = Hessian of the loss, $U$ = score covariance) gives a pointwise uncertainty on the learned log-ratio, $\sigma^2(x) = f_i(x)\, C_{ij}\, f_j(x)$, which propagates to the POI by the Gong–Samaniego pseudo-MLE result $\sigma^2_{\rm GS} = \sigma^2_{\rm MLE}\,(1 + \sigma^2_{\rm MLE} A_i C_{ij} A_j)$. That is the sense in which it is *trivial to apply*: **no bootstrap loop over the entire analysis** — the uncertainty arrives as an analytic *inflation* of the usual MLE variance.

**Read it against §13.2, because it settles an argument made there.** Plain deep ensembling (Ref. 16) averages M networks with uniform weights $1/M$ and calls their spread the model uncertainty. Benevedes & Thaler show that this **naive uniform-weight ensemble undercovers and is biased**, while *fitting* the weights and carrying their covariance reaches nominal coverage (M = 32, quark/gluon jets). Ensembling-as-averaging is not a calibration fix; ensembling-as-a-fitted-basis, with the fit's covariance propagated, can be.

**But do not adopt it uncritically here.** Two boundaries matter. (i) *Scope:* the paper is explicit that $C \sim O(1/N_{\rm fit})$ captures the noise of the finite **fitting** sample — **not** mismodelling from limited network expressivity, training stochasticity, finite *training* data, or simulator systematics. It closes one term of condition (C), not the condition. (ii) *Regime:* every guarantee is **asymptotic** (fixed M, $N_{\rm fit} \to \infty$; Gong–Samaniego is a delta-method inflation of $\sigma^2_{\rm MLE}$, which presumes the Wald picture of §7 in the first place). This project exists because that picture fails at O(100) events. So wifi is a **candidate mitigation to be tested, not a fix to be assumed** — and the test is itself the interesting experiment: what does a method whose uncertainties are derived asymptotically do in the regime where asymptotics break? It inflates variances, so it pushes in the right direction against overconfidence; whether it inflates them *enough*, and at the right places in μ, is an empirical question this project is unusually well equipped to ask (§17.3).

## 16. Deep Sets and toy-ensemble training

### 16.1 The mechanism to attack

Hypothesis 3 of §12: standard CARL training sees one large balanced sample and learns a statement about *densities* — nothing in training encodes what a six-event dataset does to a ratio estimate. The proposed cure is to train on **many pseudo-experiments**, so the network's output can depend on the dataset *as a whole*, including how big it is. That requires a network whose input is a *dataset* — an unordered, variable-size collection.

### 16.2 Why sets need a theorem

Feeding a network "a set" is not a detail: the hypothesis class must be permutation-invariant **by construction**, not by hoping augmentation teaches it. Deep Sets (Ref. 12) characterises the class exactly — a function f(X) on sets (from a countable universe) is permutation-invariant **iff** it decomposes as

$$f(X)=\rho\Big(\sum_{x\in X}\phi(x)\Big)$$

**Proof sketch, both directions, because it is short enough to own.** (⇐) A sum is blind to the order of its terms, and ρ of a permutation-invariant quantity is permutation-invariant — done. (⇒, the interesting direction) Choose φ to be an *injective multiset encoder*: for a countable universe one can send the k-th element to digits in a positional code (think: φ(x_k) = 4^{−k}), so that the **sum alone already determines the entire multiset** — which elements, with which multiplicities. Then let ρ be "decode the sum, apply f". Any permutation-invariant f is therefore of the form ρ(Σφ). In practice φ and ρ are learned networks (universal approximators standing in for encoder and decoder); the paper's fine print — for *uncountable* domains like our continuous kinematics the decomposition is proven for fixed-size sets and conjectured in general — is worth remembering but changes nothing operationally.

### 16.3 The load-bearing detail: sum, not mean

With **sum** pooling, adding events *moves* the pooled vector — ρ can read multiplicity as well as shape, so learned confidence *can* scale with N. With **mean** pooling the same network sees only shape and is structurally blind to sample size: the precise blindness being cured. (For weighted events, two natural encodings: feed φ the pair (kinematics, toy weight), or realise weights as multiplicities.) This one design bit is the entire connection between the architecture and condition (B).

![Deep Sets architecture](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/martafsilva31/phd-figures/main/sbi-shortcomings/sbi-foundations/deepsets.png)

### 16.4 Design space, and the honest caveat

Candidate outputs, in increasing ambition: (a) a per-dataset *correction* to the standard NSBI curve — learn only the finite-N distortion, safest, anchored to the existing analysis; (b) an amortised per-dataset likelihood-ratio *curve* over the μ grid; (c) the interval or statistic directly. Open issues to keep in view: generalisation across luminosities (train at several L, test between); and the set network's **own calibration** — it is a trained estimator too, so condition (C) and §13's diagnostics apply to it *recursively*.

> 💡 Note precisely what this does and does not fix. A set network that has *seen* six-event datasets can learn how uncertain six events make you — condition (B), from the estimator's side. It does **not** make its own learned mapping correct — condition (C) — which is why the natural end-state is a *combination*: a learned, sample-size-aware summary statistic, **calibrated classically with toys** via the Neyman construction of §6.2. The learning buys power; the construction buys validity. Neither alone buys both.

## 17. The research programme

Each direction below is written to be read on its own: what it is, why it should work, how one would do it here, and what each outcome would mean. The through-line is §7's condition table — every direction attacks or diagnoses a named condition.

### 17.1 Toy-ensemble training with a set architecture — attacks (B) via the estimator

**What.** Train a permutation-invariant network (§16) on unlimited toy pseudo-experiments (§3 provides a generator with μ and L varied per sample), so the analysis itself becomes a function of the whole dataset, sample size included.
**Why it should work.** It supplies, at training time, exactly the information whose absence defines hypothesis 3: the look of finite datasets. The sum-pooled representation can carry N, so the learned uncertainty can scale with the available statistics — the thing single-Asimov training structurally cannot.
**How.** Generator draws (μ_true, L), throws one toy per sample, feeds the padded/masked event set; outputs per §16.4, starting at ambition (a) or a simple (μ̂, σ) regression; evaluate coverage across the (μ_true, L) plane.
**Outcomes.** Learned uncertainty tracking the toy scatter across L = mechanism (B) is learnable → the failure was sample-size blindness. Residual miscoverage after calibration = the estimator's own condition-(C) share, isolated and measurable.

### 17.2 Corrected test statistics: Bartlett and beyond — corrects (B) at the statistic level

**What.** Rescale $t_\mu$ by its toy-ensemble mean, per hypothesis (§14); compare Asimov vs toys on corrected curves.
**Why.** Cheapest correction on the table: one scalar per (μ, L), already produced by scans we run anyway; theory promises an order in 1/n where the model is regular.
**How.** Per-hypothesis means from the existing ensembles; corrected curves; re-ask the Asimov question; check the level-dependence of what the correction achieves.
**Outcomes.** The diagnostic sentence of §14.3 verbatim: rescaling reconciles Asimov and toys → distributional failure, (B); it does not → estimator implicated, (C). Either result moves the project; that is what a good diagnostic looks like.

### 17.3 MC statistical uncertainties — closes the simulation half of (C)

**What.** Barlow–Beeston(-lite) profiling in both analyses (§15).
**Why.** Conservative by construction; removes the "your templates were finite" alibi from every other comparison; the widening concentrates exactly where occupancy is worst, so it doubles as an occupancy probe.
**How.** Per-bin γ with closed-form profile and n_eff constraints in the histogram family; for NSBI, template-side profiling of the rate/shape inputs, plus the **network side via wifi ensembles** (§15.1, Ref. 17): M frozen basis networks, weights fitted on a held-out sample, the analytic weight covariance propagated to μ by Gong–Samaniego. The April meeting's method is identified (2026-07-13) and is no longer a blocker on this direction; what it needs is a *test*, not a confirmation.
**The sharp version of the test.** wifi's uncertainties are themselves asymptotic (fixed M, N_fit → ∞), so applying them at 1–10 fb⁻¹ asks whether an asymptotically-derived inflation can repair a failure *caused by* the breakdown of asymptotics. Both outcomes are publishable: it restores coverage → the missing ingredient was ratio-estimation noise, and the cure is cheap; it does not → the overconfidence is not fitting-sample noise at all, and hypothesis 3 (sample-size blindness, §17.1) survives a serious challenge from the one method the field would reach for first.
**Outcomes.** If miscoverage persists with MC-stat profiled, finite simulation is exonerated and the remaining suspects are (A)/(B)/(C-network). If it vanishes, the entire story was template statistics — cheapest possible resolution.

### 17.4 The dimensionality study — separates (B) from (C) with no ML in sight

**What.** Add axes to a plain histogram (2D, 3D, 4D…) and scan luminosity, tracking the Asimov property as a function of cell occupancy.
**Why.** It is the decisive experiment of §8: a histogram has no learned component, so any breakdown it shows is pure condition (B). Matching NSBI's failure occupancies exonerates the network; failing to break implicates it.
**How.** Multi-dimensional binning of the same events, same toys, same comparator; report against mean and *effective* occupancy (the N_eff of §7(B), because cell population is wildly non-uniform); control at fixed total bin count to separate "dimension" from "cells".
**Outcomes.** Collapse of the mismatch onto occupancy → (B) is the mechanism, mitigations should target statistics, not networks. No mismatch even at NSBI-like occupancies → the learned estimator is the difference, (C). Direction of any mismatch is a falsification lever: an Asimov-*narrower* mismatch would contradict the founding observation's direction and force a rethink.

### 17.5 Conservative training objectives (BNRE) — attacks (C) in the loss

**What.** Add the balancing penalty of §13.3 to the CARL loss; everything else unchanged.
**Why.** The only mitigation that changes *what the network converges to*: provably conservative-in-expectation at finite budget, at zero architectural cost, and the natural baseline every fancier fix must beat.
**How.** One penalty term; scan its coefficient; evaluate with the same Asimov-vs-toys and coverage protocol.
**Outcomes.** If balance alone restores the Asimov property, the failure was classifier overconfidence in the (C) sense. If curves become *wider but still mismatched*, conservatism ≠ calibration — an important distinction to demonstrate.

### 17.6 Toy-calibrated inference: the Neyman fallback — restores validity under all conditions

**What.** Stop taking the asymptotic shortcut entirely: per hypothesis, calibrate the statistic's threshold with toys (§6.2), whatever the statistic — the classical construction wrapped around a modern statistic.
**Why.** It is the *only* item on this list whose validity does not depend on (A), (B), (C) or (D) — coverage is guaranteed by construction. The price is per-hypothesis toys and (with a poor statistic) longer intervals: validity is bought, efficiency still has to be earned.
**How.** Belts from per-hypothesis toy quantiles of any chosen statistic (the NSBI $t_\mu$, a learned summary, anything); intervals by inversion; efficiency compared across statistics; Feldman–Cousins ordering as the refinement.
**Outcomes.** This is less an experiment than the safety net under all of them — and combined with 17.1 it is the natural end-state sketched in §16.4: learned power, constructed validity.

### 17.7 The N_eff diagnostic — measures (B) directly

**What.** Compute the effective sample size of the score sum (§7(B)) per analysis, per luminosity, per toy.
**Why.** It is the representation-independent replacement for "events per bin" — a single number that should *predict* where the Asimov property fails, if (B) is the mechanism. A diagnostic that predicts is worth ten that describe.
**How.** Per-event score contributions from the existing machinery; Kish formula; plot breakdown metrics against N_eff across analyses and dimensions — histogram and NSBI on the *same axis*, which no occupancy metric can offer.
**Outcomes.** If curves from different analyses collapse on N_eff, the project has found its universal control variable — and a publishable, transferable diagnostic that any analysis can compute for itself.

## 18. Open questions

Conceptual questions this note leaves open (operational ones live in the env-setup note; result-driven ones in the Logbook):

- **Which "median of toys" is the right comparator?** §3.5 lists three inequivalent definitions; quantitative statements are not comparable across them. Which one's failure is *interesting* — and which is a convention artefact — must be settled and stated.
- **What scalar metric should quantify "Asimov-property violation"?** Curve distance, interval-width ratio, coverage error at fixed level? The project promises a breakdown metric as a function of luminosity and dimensionality; the metric itself is an unjustified choice so far.
- **How do (A), (B), (C) separate empirically?** They respond differently to luminosity — (A) worsens, (B) improves, (C) is flat. Designing the cleanest version of that separation experiment is open.
- **Where does the histogram itself break?** At what effective occupancy does a plain histogram start violating the property? That empirically locates the asymptotic boundary in a system with no network in it.
- **Can the Asimov check and expected coverage be related quantitatively?** §13.1 supplies the dictionary (ECP = prior-averaged frequentist coverage); a pooling protocol that lets both literatures' evidence bear on one number is missing.
- **Which generator produced the samples?** Not established by any file, comment, or slide — worth two minutes of Tei's time, and probably prerequisite to explaining the cross-section prefactors in the dataset normalisation.
- ~~**What is the "Wi-Fi ensemble" method?**~~ **RESOLVED 2026-07-13** — not garbled: *wᵢfᵢ ensembles*, Benevedes & Thaler (Ref. 17, arXiv:2506.00113), a pun on weights wᵢ × basis functions fᵢ. Written up in §15.1; Tei's confirmation is a courtesy, not a dependency. The question it *becomes*: **does an asymptotically-derived uncertainty inflation restore coverage in a regime defined by the failure of asymptotics?** (§17.3.)

## 19. Glossary

| Term | One line |
|------|----------|
| Asimov dataset | Artificial dataset in which every observable equals its expectation; fitting it returns the true parameters exactly. |
| Asimov property (operational, this project) | Asimov −2Δln L scan curve == pointwise-per-μ median of toy pseudo-experiment scan curves. |
| Asymptotic regime | Sample sizes and model conditions such that Wilks/Wald hold: LR statistic ~ χ², scan curves parabolic (see §7). |
| Weighted MC sample | Simulated events with weights summing to the expected yield; dense in shape, normalised in rate. |
| Toy pseudo-experiment | One Poisson draw per event weight, $w^{toy}\sim\mathcal{P}(wL)$ — an exact sample from the extended likelihood (§3.3). |
| Coverage | Fraction of repeated experiments whose reported interval contains the truth; the operational meaning of an error bar (§6). |
| Overconfidence | Coverage below the stated confidence level; intervals shorter than warranted. |
| Neyman construction | Per-hypothesis acceptance regions from toy quantiles; coverage guaranteed by construction, no asymptotics (§6.2). |
| Score | Derivative of the log-likelihood in the POI; a sum over events whose Gaussianity the CLT must deliver (§7B). |
| Effective sample size $N_{\rm eff}$ | $(\Sigma u)^2/\Sigma u^2$; the representation-independent version of "events per bin". |
| Regularity (condition A) | Non-vanishing Fisher information at the truth, identifiability, interior truth. |
| Mis-specification (condition C) | The maximised likelihood is not the true likelihood — automatic for a learned ratio, impossible for binned counts. |
| Rate fold, μ\* | The stationary point of ν(μ) at $(\sigma_I/2\sigma_S)^2$; near it the count carries no first-order μ-information (§7A). |
| Extended likelihood | Poisson of the total count × product of per-event densities. |
| Rate / shape term | The Poisson factor (analytic, exact here) / the per-event product (all the networks supply). |
| POI, μ | Signal-strength modifier: amplitude × √μ, signal rate × μ; μ = 1 is the SM. |
| SIG / BKG / INT / SBI | Signal, background, (destructive) interference, and their physical sum. |
| SBI / NSBI | (Neural) simulation-based inference: inference when the likelihood is only accessible through simulation. |
| CARL | Likelihood-ratio estimation via calibrated classifiers: $s/(1-s)=p_{\rm num}/p_{\rm ref}$, derived in §9.1. |
| Calibration (CARL sense) | Diagnosing/correcting the residual distortion of an imperfectly trained classifier's ratio. |
| Ensembling | Averaging networks differing in initialisation/training noise; estimates model variance, not data statistics (§13.2). |
| BNRE | Balanced neural ratio estimation: a loss constraint making learned ratios conservative in expectation (§13.3). |
| Bartlett correction | Rescaling the LR statistic by its finite-sample mean; whole distribution approaches χ² an order faster, for regular models (§14). |
| Barlow–Beeston (lite) | Per-bin nuisance parameters for finite-MC template uncertainty, constrained by $n_{\rm eff}$ (§15). |
| Deep Sets | Permutation-invariant architecture $\rho(\Sigma\phi)$ — the unique general form (§16.2). |
| Sum vs mean pooling | Sum carries multiplicity (sample size visible); mean is shape-only (§16.3). |
